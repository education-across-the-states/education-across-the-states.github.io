The Oregon IEP Guide for Parents

A step-by-step guide to special education in Oregon — written for the parent doing this at 11 PM

Education Across the States · Charles Mayfield, Producer Current through November 5, 2026 — next scheduled review July 2027

How to use this guide

An IEP — an Individualized Education Program — is the written plan that spells out what special education and related services a school district must provide to a child with a disability. It is a legal document, not a favor. When a district agrees to something in an IEP, it has to do it.

This guide walks the whole road in order: how a child gets evaluated, how the IEP gets built, how you stay in charge of it year after year, and what to do when something goes wrong. Oregon runs special education on its own statutes (ORS Chapter 343) and its own rules (OAR Chapter 581, Division 15), and several of Oregon's rules differ from the federal defaults you will read about in national guides — sometimes better for families, sometimes simply different. Where Oregon's rule differs from the federal rule, this guide says so. Where a rule is federal, it says that too.

You do not need to read it cover to cover. If you are at a specific step tonight, go straight to that step.

Terms used throughout: District means your local school district. ESD means your Education Service District — Oregon's regional service agencies. Districts deliver most school-age special education themselves, but many buy specialized services through their ESD, and Oregon's youngest children (birth to kindergarten) are served through ESDs and their contractors. ODE is the Oregon Department of Education, in Salem. FAPE means a free appropriate public education — the legal standard everything in this guide is measured against.

Part 1 — The basics

What an IEP is

Federal law — the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) — gives every eligible child with a disability the right to a free appropriate public education in the least restrictive environment. Oregon law builds on that federal floor: ORS Chapter 343 sets the state's own procedures, and the State Board of Education's rules in OAR 581-015-2000 through 581-015-2990 carry the details — timelines, consent rules, hearing procedures, even the state's own standard IEP form. This guide covers both layers and labels each one.

Who is covered

For school-age children, Oregon districts must provide FAPE to every child with a disability who has reached age 5 but not yet reached 21 on or before September 1 of the current school year (OAR 581-015-2040). A student whose 21st birthday falls during the school year stays eligible for the rest of that year. Two things end eligibility: turning 21 (as above) and receiving a regular high school diploma — a certificate or a GED does not end it (OAR 581-015-2045).

For younger children: Early Intervention (birth to age 3) and Early Childhood Special Education (age 3 to kindergarten entry) are provided through your ESD or its contractors under an Individualized Family Service Plan (IFSP), not an IEP. ODE publishes a separate procedural safeguards notice for EI/ECSE families. If your child is approaching kindergarten, the move from an IFSP to a district IEP is a planned transition — start asking about it months ahead, not weeks.

Oregon's disability categories (OAR 581-015-2130 through 581-015-2180, and ORS 343.035) are: intellectual disability; deafness or being hard of hearing; speech or language impairment; visual impairment, including blindness; deafblindness; emotional behavior disability; orthopedic impairment; other health impairment; autism spectrum disorder; traumatic brain injury; specific learning disability; and developmental delay for children ages 3 through 9.

IEP or 504?

A child who needs specially designed instruction because of a disability needs an IEP. A child who can learn with accommodations — changes to how they access learning, not what they are taught — may be served by a Section 504 plan instead. The two laws are different, the documents are different, and the dispute systems are different (in Oregon, they are more different than most states — see the companion guide). Our Oregon 504 Guide for Parents covers that road. One rule of thumb: if the school is evaluating your child, it should be considering both.

The school has a duty to find your child

Districts cannot wait for parents to figure this out. Oregon law (ORS 343.157; OAR 581-015-2080) imposes a Child Find duty: districts must identify, locate, and evaluate children who may have disabilities and need special education — including children who are passing their classes, children in private school, children who are homeless or highly mobile, and children whose difficulties show up as behavior rather than grades. If you suspect a disability, you do not have to prove it before asking for an evaluation. Suspicion is the trigger. Step 1 starts there.

Part 2 — Getting evaluated

Step 1: Ask for an evaluation — in writing

What this means. Nothing in Oregon special education starts until someone refers a child for evaluation. A teacher can refer. So can you, at any time, for any child you reasonably suspect has a disability affecting their education.

What to do.

  1. Write a short letter or email to your child's teacher, principal, or the district's special education director. Say: "I am requesting that my child be evaluated for special education eligibility in all areas of suspected disability." Name what you are seeing — reading, speech, attention, behavior, motor skills, social development — in plain words.
  2. Date it. Keep a copy. Email creates its own paper trail; if you hand-deliver, ask for a date-stamped copy.
  3. Do not accept "let's wait and see" or "we have to finish intervention tiers first" as a substitute for an evaluation. Intervention programs can run alongside an evaluation; they are not a legal waiting room in front of one.

Oregon specifics. Oregon law does not give the district a separate number of days to respond to your referral with an evaluation plan — Oregon folds planning into the team process described in Step 2, and Oregon's one big evaluation clock (Step 3) starts at your written consent, not at your request. That makes your paper trail doubly important: your dated request, and the date you sign consent, are the two dates everything else is counted from. If the district refuses to evaluate, it must tell you why in writing (prior written notice, Step 12) — a refusal you can challenge in Part 5.

Watch out for: a verbal "we'll look into it" that never becomes a consent form. No consent form means the 60-school-day clock has not started — and cannot be enforced. If weeks pass after your written request with no consent form offered, write again and say so.

Step 2: Evaluation planning — the team's first job, with you on it

What this means. Before any testing, Oregon requires evaluation planning (OAR 581-015-2115): the team — the IEP team and other qualified professionals, with your input — reviews the information that already exists about your child: classroom assessments and observations, teacher and provider observations, and evaluations and information you provide. Based on that review, the team decides what additional data, if any, is needed to answer four questions: Is your child a child with a disability? What are your child's present levels and needs? Does your child need special education? And (at reevaluation) what additions or changes do the services need?

What to do.

  1. Bring your information to the planning step — report cards, work samples, private evaluations, medical letters, your own written observations. The rule names parent-provided information first in the list of data to review; make sure yours is on the table.
  2. Check the plan against your concerns. Every area you named in Step 1 that the plan leaves out is a question to ask in writing: "Please add assessment in [area], because [what you observe]." The evaluation must cover all areas related to the suspected disability — health, vision, hearing, social and emotional status, general intelligence, academic performance, communication, motor abilities (OAR 581-015-2110).
  3. If the district holds a planning meeting, you must be invited to participate (OAR 581-015-2115(2)). (The review itself may be done without a meeting — but your input is required either way.)

Oregon specifics — consent. No evaluation happens until the district has given you prior written notice describing what it proposes and obtained your informed written consent (OAR 581-015-2090). Consent means you were fully informed in your native language or other mode of communication, and that you agree in writing. Consent for the evaluation is not consent for services — signing it does not enroll your child in special education or change any class (OAR 581-015-2090(3)(a)(A)).

If you refuse an initial evaluation, Oregon lets the district choose to pursue it anyway through mediation or a due process hearing — but the district is not required to, and it does not violate Child Find if it declines (OAR 581-015-2090(3)(b)). Initial services are different: if you refuse or don't respond, the district may not override you, may not provide the services, and is not violating FAPE by withholding them (OAR 581-015-2090(4)). Those two rules are not symmetrical, and Step 9 comes back to them.

Watch out for: an evaluation plan built only from the school's data, with your information never reviewed. The rule requires the review to include what parents provide — if your private evaluation or your written concerns were never considered, say so in writing before consenting.

Step 3: The evaluation — and Oregon's 60 school-day clock

What this means. Assessors observe your child, review records, talk to teachers, and test in the areas the planning step identified. Then the team meets to decide eligibility. (Eligibility is Step 5; it happens inside this same clock.)

Here is the Oregon difference most national guides get wrong. Federal law's familiar figure is "60 days" — calendar days — from consent to a finished evaluation. Oregon counts school days. Under OAR 581-015-2110(5)(a), an initial evaluation must be completed within 60 school days from your written consent to the date of the meeting to consider eligibility. Reevaluation runs on the same 60-school-day clock (OAR 581-015-2110(5)(b)). Sixty school days is roughly twelve school weeks — on a calendar, usually longer than 60 days, and it stretches across breaks because non-school days don't count. Plan your expectations, and your calendar, accordingly.

The documented exceptions (OAR 581-015-2110(5)(c)) — the clock may run longer only when one of these is documented in your child's record:

  • Your child transfers to a new district mid-evaluation, the new district is making sufficient progress to finish promptly, and you and the district agree in writing to a specific completion date;
  • A parent repeatedly fails or refuses to produce the child for the evaluation; or
  • You and the district agree in writing to extend the timeline for a specific learning disability evaluation.

What to do.

  1. Write down your consent date. Count 60 school days on the school calendar — instructional days, not weekends, holidays, or breaks. That date is the deadline for the eligibility meeting, not just for testing to finish.
  2. Ask for copies of the evaluation reports before the eligibility meeting so you can read them at your kitchen table, not across a conference table. You are entitled to a copy of the evaluation report and the eligibility documentation.
  3. If a medical, vision, or hearing exam is part of the evaluation, Oregon law requires it be done by a licensed practitioner — physician, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant for medical exams; a licensed optometrist or ophthalmologist for vision; a licensed audiologist for hearing (ORS 343.146(5)–(7)).
  4. If you disagree with the district's evaluation when it is done, read Step 4 before the meeting ends.

Watch out for: the eligibility meeting scheduled at school day 59 with reports handed to you at the door — or a "draft" eligibility decision presented as settled. Ask to reschedule within the window with reports in hand ahead of time, or state on the record that you are proceeding without adequate time to review, and note it in the meeting notes.

Step 4: If you disagree with the evaluation — the independent evaluation (IEE)

What this means. If the district's evaluation gets your child wrong — wrong tests, wrong conclusions, areas missed — you have the right to an Independent Educational Evaluation by a qualified examiner who does not work for the district, at public expense: the district pays. Oregon states the right twice — in statute (ORS 343.173(2)) and in rule (OAR 581-015-2305).

What to do.

  1. Say so, in writing, and say exactly which evaluation you disagree with. (Our IEE request letter does this.) You do not have to explain why — the district may ask, but you are not required to answer, and it may not use your silence to delay (OAR 581-015-2305(6)).
  2. Ask the district for its IEE criteria and information about where to obtain an IEE — the district must provide that information on request (ORS 343.173(4); OAR 581-015-2305(2)).
  3. The district then has exactly two lawful options: fund the IEE, or file for a due process hearing to prove its own evaluation was appropriate. There is no third option of ignoring you.

The rule, stated exactly. Oregon's rule (OAR 581-015-2305(4)) requires the district to act "without unnecessary delay." There is no number of days — not five, not fifteen. Some national template letters invent a deadline here; do not rely on one, and do not let a district hide behind one either. What the law forbids is unnecessary delay: if weeks pass with no funded IEE and no hearing filed, that is the violation, and your dated request letter is the proof.

You may obtain one IEE at public expense each time the district conducts an evaluation you disagree with. You may also pay for your own independent evaluation at any time; either way, the team must consider the results, and they may be presented as evidence at a hearing. Oregon also protects the edges of the right: the district's IEE criteria (location, examiner qualifications, cost) must be the same criteria the district uses for its own evaluations; beyond those criteria the district may not impose conditions or timelines on obtaining an IEE at public expense; and you must be given the opportunity to show that unique circumstances justify an IEE that does not meet the district's criteria (OAR 581-015-2305(3)). If a hearing officer orders an IEE as part of a hearing, it is at public expense (ORS 343.173(5)).

Watch out for: "We'll just re-test." A second district evaluation is not an IEE. And a district that says it will fund an IEE but applies criteria so narrow that no qualified examiner qualifies — that is a dispute for Part 5.

Step 5: Eligibility — a team decision, signed by everyone

What this means. Oregon's eligibility decision is made by a defined team: you, plus two or more qualified professionals, at least one of whom is knowledgeable and experienced in evaluating and educating children with the suspected disability (OAR 581-015-2120(1)). The team works from the evaluation report and must answer, in a written statement of eligibility:

  • What evaluation data the team considered;
  • Whether your child meets the criteria for one of Oregon's disability categories (OAR 581-015-2130 through 581-015-2180, or developmental delay under 581-015-2127);
  • Whether the primary basis for the suspected disability is instead a lack of appropriate instruction in reading or math, or limited English proficiency — either of which rules out eligibility;
  • Whether the disability has an adverse impact on your child's educational performance; and
  • Whether, because of the disability, your child needs special education services.

Every team member signs the statement, indicating agreement or disagreement with the determination (OAR 581-015-2120(2)(b)(F)).

What to do.

  1. Read the written statement before you leave. If you disagree with the outcome, your signature records your disagreement — that is the system working as designed, not a confrontation.
  2. If your child is found ineligible, ask for the reasoning in the statement and for prior written notice: the district must explain in writing what it is refusing, why, what data it relied on, and what other options were considered (OAR 581-015-2310).
  3. If the team says "not eligible for an IEP," ask the 504 question in the same meeting: "Will the school evaluate for a Section 504 plan?" Many children who do not need specialized instruction still qualify for accommodations. See the companion 504 guide.
  4. If you disagree with an ineligibility decision, your options are the IEE (Step 4) and the dispute ladder (Part 5).

Watch out for: "Their grades are fine, so they don't qualify." Oregon's test is disability + adverse impact on educational performance + need for special education. Educational performance is broader than grades — it includes functional, social, behavioral, and communication performance. A team that looked only at report cards did not apply Oregon's standard.

Part 3 — Developing the IEP

Step 6: The IEP meeting — your seat at the table

What this means. If your child is eligible, the IEP is written by a team: you, at least one of your child's general education teachers, at least one special education teacher or provider, a district representative who can commit resources, someone who can explain the evaluation results, and — when transition is on the table — your child and invited agency representatives (OAR 581-015-2210).

Your rights at the table (know these cold):

  • Written notice of the meeting, early enough for you to attend, stating the purpose, time, and place, who will attend — and telling you two things districts sometimes omit: that you may invite others who know your child or have special expertise, and that the team may proceed without you if you don't attend (OAR 581-015-2190(2)).
  • A mutually agreed time and place. The district must schedule at a time and place you agree to; if neither parent can attend, it must offer other ways to participate, including individual or conference phone calls or home visits (OAR 581-015-2195(1)–(2)). A meeting may be held without you only after documented attempts — records of calls, copies of correspondence, records of home visits (OAR 581-015-2195(3)).
  • An interpreter if you are deaf or your native language is not English — the district must take whatever action is necessary to ensure you understand the proceedings (OAR 581-015-2190(3)).
  • Bring someone with you — a family member, a friend, an advocate, an attorney. The notice itself must tell you this right.
  • A copy of the IEP at no cost to you (OAR 581-015-2195(5)).
  • Take the draft home. Oregon IEPs are written on the Oregon Standard IEP form (Step 7). You do not have to signal agreement in the room. Read it overnight. If a signature line confuses attendance with consent, write next to your signature what you mean: "Signed to show attendance only — consent decision to follow in writing."

Oregon specifics — the 30-day meeting clock. Oregon's rule (OAR 581-015-2220(2)(a)) requires the district to hold the meeting to develop the initial IEP within 30 calendar days of the determination that your child needs special education — and to make services available as soon as possible after the IEP is developed. Note the calendar asymmetry that defines Oregon practice: you waited 60 school days for the evaluation and eligibility decision; the IEP meeting itself is due within 30 calendar days of that decision.

Watch out for: the completed IEP presented as a done deal — "we already drafted this, just sign." Drafts are legal; predetermination is not. The team, with you in it, must be able to change the document. If every page is final before discussion starts, say so in the meeting and in writing afterward.

Step 7: What the IEP must contain

Oregon's content rule (OAR 581-015-2200) requires:

  1. Present levels — your child's current academic achievement and functional performance, including how the disability affects involvement and progress in the general education curriculum.
  2. Measurable annual goals — academic and functional (with short-term objectives for students who take alternate assessments), designed to meet the needs from the disability and enable progress in the general curriculum.
  3. How progress will be measured and when you will be told — the IEP must describe the measurement method and the periodic reports (for example, reports concurrent with report cards).
  4. Special education and related services — the specific services, supplementary aids, and program modifications or staff supports, based on peer-reviewed research to the extent practicable.
  5. Dates, amounts, places — the projected start date and the anticipated frequency, amount, location, and duration of each service. Vague language ("as needed," "consultation available") is not a service commitment.
  6. Participation with nondisabled children — an explanation of the extent, if any, your child will not participate in the regular class and activities. Oregon starts (as federal law does) from the least restrictive environment.
  7. Assessment accommodations — how your child participates in state and district-wide assessments, or why the alternate assessment is appropriate. A child may not be exempted from state assessment because of a disability unless the parent has requested an exemption under OAR 581-022-0612.
  8. Transition planning — see Step 8.
  9. The rights-transfer statement — beginning at least one year before your child turns 18, a statement that the district has informed your child that rights will transfer at 18 (Step 18).

Oregon specifics — it is literally a standard form. Every Oregon district must develop, review, and revise IEPs using the Oregon Standard IEP form, unless ODE has approved an alternate form for that district (OAR 581-015-2215; ORS 343.151). That means the document in your hands looks the same in Portland as in Lakeview — and an IEP missing a section of the standard form is missing something the form itself requires.

What to do. Read the services page like a contract — because it is one. Every service your child was promised in the meeting should appear with minutes/frequency. If it was said in the room and is not in the document, it does not exist.

Step 8: Transition planning — Oregon starts at 16, and you can ask for 14

What this means. Transition planning is the IEP's bridge to adult life: measurable goals for education/training, employment, and (where appropriate) independent living after high school, plus the services and courses of study that lead there, built on age-appropriate transition assessments of your child's strengths, preferences, and interests.

Oregon specifics — an honest account. Oregon follows the federal start age: transition services must be in the IEP beginning not later than the first IEP in effect when your child turns 16 (OAR 581-015-2200(2)(a)). Oregon does not start earlier by default — some states do. But Oregon's rule expressly allows the team to start as early as age 14 or younger, if the IEP team — including you — determines that is appropriate, and the plan is updated annually. If your 14- or 15-year-old needs transition planning now, the rule is your citation for asking.

One genuinely Oregon requirement: employment planning must include information about supported employment services provided by Oregon Vocational Rehabilitation and the Oregon Office of Developmental Disabilities Services, delivered in competitive, integrated employment settings — and the district must give that information to you, the parent, at each annual review once transition planning is in effect. If VR and ODDS have never been mentioned at your child's annual review, ask why.

Watch out for: a transition plan whose "goals" are generic ("will explore careers"). Measurable postsecondary goals, tied to transition assessments of your child, are the legal standard. And when a meeting will address transition, the meeting notice must say so, must state that your child will be invited, and must identify any other agency invited to send a representative (OAR 581-015-2195(4)).

Step 9: Signing — consent, and what it does and doesn't do

What this means. Oregon law treats three consents differently, and confusing them causes real harm:

  • Consent to evaluate (Step 2) — permission to test. Never consent to services or placement.
  • Consent to initial services. The district must obtain your informed consent before first providing special education (OAR 581-015-2090(4)). If you refuse, don't respond, or revoke consent for initial services, the district may not use mediation or a hearing to override you; it is not violating FAPE by not serving your child; and it need not even convene an IEP meeting for the services you declined. No services begin without your yes.
  • Revoking consent later. After services have begun, you may revoke consent for continued services — in writing. The district must give you prior written notice, then stop. It may not keep serving your child, and it does not have to erase the record that services were provided (OAR 581-015-2090(4)(e)). Revocation is all-or-nothing: it ends special education and its protections, including the discipline protections in Step 16. Do not sign a revocation in a heated meeting. Take it home.

Watch out for: services starting late because "the paperwork wasn't final." Once the IEP is developed, services must be made available as soon as possible (OAR 581-015-2220(2)(b)). A start date belongs in the document; hold the district to it.

Part 4 — Staying involved

Step 10: The IEP year — monitoring, annual review, reevaluation

What this means. An IEP is not a one-time event. Progress reports come to you on the schedule written in the IEP. The team must review the IEP periodically, and at least once every 365 days (Oregon's annual-review rule, OAR 581-015-2225), and your child must be reevaluated at least once every three years unless you and the district agree in writing that a reevaluation is unnecessary (federal rule, 34 CFR 300.303). An Oregon reevaluation runs on the same 60-school-day clock as the initial evaluation (OAR 581-015-2110(5)(b)). You can request an IEP meeting at any time — put the request in writing and keep the date.

What to do.

  1. Keep an IEP binder or folder: every IEP, every assessment, every progress report, every email. One folder, chronological.
  2. Compare each progress report against the goal's own measurement method. "Making progress" without data is an opinion; the IEP promised measurement.
  3. Watch the triennial date and the annual review date the way you watched the 60-school-day clock. Both are your child's protection against a stale plan.

Oregon specifics — records in 10 business days. Oregon gives you a records right stronger than the federal default, in both statute and rule: you are entitled at any reasonable time to examine all district records about your child's identification, evaluation, placement, and FAPE, and records must be provided without undue delay — which may not exceed 10 business days from your request (ORS 343.173(1); OAR 581-015-2300(3)). Copies must arrive before any IEP meeting, due process hearing, or resolution session about your child. Records may be redacted only as needed to protect other children's information. (For comparison: the federal FERPA default allows schools up to 45 days. Oregon's rule is 10 business days.)

Watch out for: the annual review held without you because "it was just routine." There is no routine exception. You are a required member of the team, and a meeting without proper notice and a genuine opportunity to participate is a procedural violation — Part 5 territory.

Step 11: You are not alone — Oregon's parent center

  • FACT Oregon is Oregon's Parent Training and Information Center (PTI) — designated by both the U.S. Department of Education and the Oregon Department of Education. FACT Oregon is a family-led nonprofit that has supported Oregon families since 2002: training, a helpline, help preparing for meetings, and plain-language toolkits (including one built with ODE that walks through Oregon's state complaint process step by step). Its services are free. factoregon.org.
  • Your ESD. Education Service Districts run Early Intervention and Early Childhood Special Education and many low-incidence services (through Regional Inclusive Services), and ESD staff often sit in district IEP meetings for the services they deliver. Knowing which agency actually provides a service tells you whose records to request and who answers for it in Part 5.
  • ODE's Dispute Resolution Section — even before you file anything, ODE's team answers questions about mediation, complaints, and due process: ode.disputeresolution@ode.oregon.gov · (503) 947-5797.
  • The State Advisory Council for Special Education (SACSE) (ORS 343.287) advises ODE on unmet needs. Hearing decisions are sent to the Council and made public, with personal information removed (OAR 581-015-2375(4)) — Oregon's due process outcomes are a public record you can learn from.

Part 5 — Rights and disputes

Disagreements are normal in special education. The system has a ladder of options, from a phone call to a courtroom, and you are allowed to start low and climb. Going up the ladder does not require finishing the rung below — but the low rungs are faster, cheaper, and often work. Every option below is described as Oregon actually runs it.

Step 12: The first moves — put it in writing, call the meeting

What to do.

  1. Write to the district describing the problem and the fix you want. Ask for prior written notice if the district is refusing something: before a district proposes or refuses to initiate or change identification, evaluation, placement, or FAPE, it must give you written notice explaining the action, why, the data behind it, the options considered, and your procedural safeguards (OAR 581-015-2310; ORS 343.159). The notice must be in your native language or other mode of communication.
  2. Request an IEP meeting to address the problem. Many disputes are IEP problems wearing a disguise — the wrong service minutes, an unimplemented accommodation, a stale goal — and the meeting is where the document can be fixed.
  3. Request your records (10 business days, ORS 343.173) before any meeting or filing. Decide from documents, not memories.

Step 13: Mediation — free, through ODE

What this means. Mediation is a structured conversation with a trained, impartial mediator who helps you and the district reach your own agreement. Oregon's rule is generous: the Oregon Department of Education offers mediation at no cost to the parties (OAR 581-015-2335(1)) — to resolve disputes including matters arising before any complaint or hearing request is filed. You do not need a case open to ask for a mediator.

The terms, as Oregon's rule sets them:

  • Mediation is voluntary for both sides, and it may not be used to deny or delay your right to a due process hearing or a state complaint.
  • Mediators are qualified and impartial, trained in effective mediation techniques, and drawn from a list ODE maintains of people knowledgeable in special education law.
  • If you reach agreement, it is set out in a legally binding written mediation agreement, signed by you and a district representative with authority to bind the district — and it is enforceable in state or federal court.
  • Mediation discussions are confidential and may not be used as evidence in a later hearing or civil proceeding.

How to ask. Use ODE's Request for Mediation form (on ODE's Mediation page) or write to the Dispute Resolution Section: ode.disputeresolution@ode.oregon.gov · (503) 947-5797. ODE also publishes a Mediation Handbook and the mediators' biographies — read them; knowing your mediator's background is free preparation.

When to use it. When the relationship still works but the decision doesn't. And know about Oregon's second neutral room: once a due process case is filed, the parties can ask for an ALJ settlement conference — a settlement process facilitated by an administrative law judge — or an advisory opinion, in which an ALJ hears a brief presentation and gives a nonbinding read of the case (ODE, Due Process Overview). Both exist to end cases early. Ask your ALJ or ODE about them.

Step 14: The state complaint — ODE investigates, within 60 days

What this means. If the district violated special education law — missed a timeline, failed to implement an IEP, skipped a required procedure — you (or anyone) may file a written, signed complaint with the Oregon Department of Education, and ODE must investigate and issue a written decision (OAR 581-015-2030).

What to do.

  1. Write a signed complaint stating: the violation, the facts behind it, your contact information, and (for a specific child) the child's name, address, school, and your proposed solution. The violation must have occurred not more than one year before ODE receives the complaint. ODE publishes a model form — the Request for Complaint Investigation (Form 581-1337-E), in English and Spanish — and a complaint Q&A and timelines sheet. You may use the form or your own letter.
  2. Send a copy to the district (or ESD/program) at the same time you file with ODE — this is required (OAR 581-015-2030(2)).
  3. File with: Oregon Department of Education, Office of Student Services — Dispute Resolution Section, 255 Capitol Street NE, Salem, OR 97310 · (503) 947-5797 · ode.disputeresolution@ode.oregon.gov.
  4. The district must respond to ODE's request within ten business days, sending you the non-confidential parts of its response. You get the opportunity to submit additional information.
  5. ODE issues a written decision addressing each allegation — findings of fact, conclusions, and reasons — within 60 days of receiving the complaint. The timeline can be extended only for exceptional circumstances, or if you and the district agree in writing to extend it to try mediation or local resolution.

Oregon specifics. If ODE finds a violation, its order includes corrective action — and where a district failed to provide appropriate services, the order must address remediation, including compensatory education or monetary reimbursement where appropriate (OAR 581-015-2030(13)). Two Oregon features deserve emphasis. First, retaliation for filing or participating in a complaint is itself a violation you can complain about (OAR 581-015-2030(19)). Second, Oregon gives both sides an after-decision path: either party may ask the Superintendent to reconsider the final order within 60 days (OAR 581-015-2030(14)(b)), and a party may seek judicial review of the order under ORS 183.484 — filed within 60 days of service, in Marion County Circuit Court or the circuit court of the county where the party resides (OAR 581-015-2030(14)(a)). Corrective action is not paused while review is sought.

A state complaint is free, needs no lawyer, and can be filed by any individual or organization — including from another state. ODE publishes a log of every complaint filed since 2008 and the orders issued: Oregon's complaint history is a public record.

Watch out for: the one-year window. It runs from the violation, not from when you learned the rule. When in doubt, file.

Step 15: Due process — the hearing

What this means. Due process is the formal legal proceeding: a party files a hearing request, an administrative law judge (ALJ) from Oregon's Office of Administrative Hearings (OAH) hears evidence, and the ALJ issues a binding decision. Oregon assigns its special education hearings to OAH under an interagency agreement between ODE and OAH (OAR 581-015-2340(1)). Parents and districts can both file. This is the route for disagreements about identification, evaluation, placement, or FAPE itself — the merits, not just the procedures.

How filing works in Oregon (the details matter):

  • Your request must name the child, the child's address (or contact information for a homeless child), and the school; describe the problem, including the facts; and state a proposed resolution, as far as you know one (OAR 581-015-2345(1)). ODE publishes a model form — the Due Process Hearing Request Information Sheet and Model Form — but any writing with the required contents works.
  • The request is filed with the school district AND the Oregon Department of Education (Office of Student Services, Dispute Resolution, 255 Capitol Street NE, Salem, OR 97310 · (503) 947-5797). "Filing" means the date ODE receives it; day-counting starts the next calendar day, and a deadline falling on a weekend or legal holiday rolls to the next business day (ODE model form).
  • Filing window: a hearing must be requested within two years after the date of the act or omission you are challenging (ORS 343.165(2); OAR 581-015-2345(3)). The exceptions are narrow: the clock does not run against a parent who was prevented from filing by the district's specific misrepresentations that it had resolved the problem, or by the district withholding information it was required to provide.
  • The district answers fast: if the district has not already sent prior written notice on the issue, it must send you a written response within ten days explaining its position, the options considered, and the data it relied on; the other party must also respond to the issues raised within ten days (OAR 581-015-2345(2)).

The timeline, as it actually runs:

  1. Resolution meeting: within 15 days of the district receiving your request, the district must convene a resolution session with you and the relevant team members — unless both sides agree in writing to waive it, or agree to use mediation instead. The district's attorney may not attend unless yours does (OAR 581-015-2355(1)).
  2. Resolution period: 30 days from the district's receipt of your request. A signed resolution agreement is legally binding (with a 3-business-day window in which either party may void it, federal rule).
  3. Hearing and decision: if the 30 days pass without resolution, the 45-day hearing timeline begins. The ALJ's decision must be entered and mailed not later than 45 days after the resolution period expires, unless the ALJ grants an extension at a party's request (OAR 581-015-2375(2); ORS 343.167).
  4. Evidence discipline: each side may prohibit evidence the other side did not disclose at least five business days before the hearing — two business days in expedited cases (ORS 343.165(4)–(5)). Calendar it the day you file.
  5. Stay put: while the case (and any appeal) is pending, your child generally remains in the then-current educational placement unless you and the district agree otherwise (ORS 343.177). File before a disputed change takes effect whenever you can.
  6. Appeal: either party may bring a civil action in state or federal court — Oregon requires it to be commenced within 90 days of the hearing officer's final order (ORS 343.175). Prevailing parents may recover reasonable attorneys' fees (ORS 343.175; federal rule).

Practical notes. You may represent yourself; many parents do, and many hire a special education attorney or advocate for this stage. ODE's Due Process Overview page maintains a list of attorneys who represent parents and a list of low-cost legal services in Oregon, and publishes the log of every hearing request filed since 2008 with the orders issued. Judges decide on the record — your binder from Step 10 and your dated letters from Steps 1–4 are the evidence. Filing does not cancel the other rungs: mediation remains available after filing, and Oregon's ALJ settlement conference (Step 13) exists precisely because most cases settle.

Discipline cases are fast-tracked: expedited hearings (Step 16) run on a compressed calendar — resolution meeting within 7 days, hearing within 20 school days, decision within 10 school days after the hearing (OAR 581-015-2445).

Step 16: Discipline — the protections that travel with the IEP

What this means. A child with an IEP can be disciplined — but the law builds a floor under the process, because removal from school is also removal from FAPE. Oregon's discipline rules are at OAR 581-015-2400 through 581-015-2445.

  • Short removals. A district may remove a child for up to 10 consecutive school days for a code violation, to the extent the same removal applies to nondisabled students (OAR 581-015-2405). Additional removals of up to 10 school days each, for separate incidents, are allowed — so long as they do not form a pattern (OAR 581-015-2410). A pattern is judged on the total days, the similarity of the behavior, the length of each removal, and how close together they came.
  • When removals become a change of placement — more than 10 consecutive days, or a pattern totaling more than 10 cumulative days in a school year — the protections below attach (OAR 581-015-2415). During further removals, services must continue so your child can keep participating in the general curriculum and progressing toward IEP goals.
  • The manifestation determination. Within 10 school days of the decision to change placement for a code violation, the district, you, and the relevant IEP team members must review all relevant information — the IEP, teacher observations, and information you provide — and answer two questions (OAR 581-015-2420): Was the behavior caused by, or directly and substantially related to, the disability? Or was it the direct result of the district's failure to implement the IEP?
  • If yes to either: the behavior is a manifestation. The district must conduct a functional behavioral assessment (unless one was already done before the behavior) and implement a behavior intervention plan (or review and modify the existing one), and your child returns to the prior placement — unless you and the district agree otherwise, or the narrow exceptions below apply (OAR 581-015-2415(4)). If the cause was the district's failure to implement the IEP, the district must take immediate steps to remedy the failure (OAR 581-015-2420(3)).
  • If no: the district may apply the same discipline as for nondisabled students — but education does not stop. Services continue during the removal so your child keeps participating in the general curriculum and progressing on IEP goals.
  • The narrow exceptions. For drug or weapon violations, or inflicting serious bodily injury at school, the district may remove your child to an interim alternative educational setting for up to 45 school days, regardless of the manifestation answer (OAR 581-015-2425). Separately, an ALJ may order a 45-day interim placement if keeping the current placement is substantially likely to result in injury (OAR 581-015-2430). Services continue in the interim setting (OAR 581-015-2435).
  • Not yet eligible? A child without an IEP can still claim these protections if the district had a basis of knowledge the child may have a disability — because you expressed concern in writing to supervisory or administrative personnel or a teacher, because you requested an evaluation, or because a teacher or other personnel expressed specific concerns about a pattern of behavior directly to the special education director or supervisory personnel (OAR 581-015-2440). Another reason Step 1 is in writing.
  • Disputes are expedited. If you disagree with a manifestation determination or a disciplinary placement, you may request an expedited due process hearing: resolution meeting within 7 days, hearing within 20 school days, decision within 10 school days after the hearing (OAR 581-015-2445; Step 15).

Watch out for: informal removals — "come pick your child up early," repeated shortened days, "home instruction" without an IEP meeting. Days your child is sent home are removal days whether or not the district calls them suspension. Count them, in writing. And if the shortened days are becoming a schedule, read Step 17 — Oregon law treats that pattern as its own legal problem, with its own remedy.

Step 17: Oregon's abbreviated-school-day law — a shortened day requires your written consent

What this means. Oregon has seen this movie: students with disabilities placed on shortened school days for months or years, sometimes for the school's convenience. In 2023 the legislature shut the practice down (ORS 343.321–343.331). This step is Oregon-only — there is no federal equivalent.

Oregon specifics — the rules, as the statute sets them.

  • A district may not unilaterally place a student with a disability on an abbreviated school day program — at any age (ORS 343.322).
  • Your child is entitled to meaningful access to the same number of hours of instruction and educational services as the majority of students in the same grade in the resident district — unless you (or a foster parent) give informed, written consent to an abbreviated program (ORS 343.322).
  • A district may not consider, recommend, or implement an abbreviated program because of inadequate staffing (ORS 343.322). "We don't have the staff" is an unlawful reason, full stop.
  • "Abbreviated school day" is counted honestly: a day the district asks your child not to attend, bars attendance, or makes attendance inaccessible — including field trips, special events, and outdoor school — can count (ORS 343.322).
  • If a program is in place with your consent, the IEP team must meet to review it 25 to 35 calendar days after placement, then at least once every 30 calendar days (and never less often than once every 90 calendar days for a student with an IEP, even if you consented to meet less often), and within 14 calendar days whenever you request a meeting (ORS 343.326).
  • You may revoke consent at any time, in writing. Revocation (or a written objection) obliges the district to restore full access; if you object in writing at least 14 calendar days before a new school year, full access resumes on day one (ORS 343.328). Complaints about violations go to ODE — and a superintendent's failure to restore access can be grounds for discipline by the Teacher Standards and Practices Commission (ORS 343.328).
  • The state counts it: Oregon requires districts to report every student with an IEP, a 504 plan, or an evaluation in progress who is placed on an abbreviated school day program for more than 10 school days in a school year, in ODE's Abbreviated Day Collection (reporting period July 1–June 30).

What to do. If your child is on a shortened schedule tonight, ask three questions in writing: Where is my signed, informed consent? When was the last IEP-team review meeting, and when is the next? And what is the plan — with dates — to restore full access? If there is no consent on file, the placement is unilateral and unlawful; say so, in writing, and cite ORS 343.322.

Step 18: If you are considering a private placement

What this means. Parents who believe the district cannot provide FAPE sometimes enroll a child in a private school and seek reimbursement. The law allows a hearing officer or court to order reimbursement — but notice rules decide many of these cases before the merits are reached.

Oregon specifics. To protect a reimbursement claim, tell the IEP team at the most recent IEP meeting you attended that you reject the proposed placement and intend to enroll privately at public expense — or give the district written notice at least 10 business days before removing your child (OAR 581-015-2515). Miss the notice and reimbursement can be reduced or denied even if you win on FAPE. The rule has protective exceptions (for example, where compliance would likely cause physical harm, or the district prevented you from giving notice) — this is a step to take with a special education attorney involved from the start.

Step 19: Age 18 — rights transfer

When your child turns 18 — the age of majority in Oregon — educational rights transfer from you to your child; the same transfer happens on marriage or emancipation, whichever comes first (OAR 581-015-2325; ORS 343.181). Beginning at least one year before the transfer, your child's IEP must carry a statement that the district has informed your child the rights will transfer (OAR 581-015-2200(2)(b)). The rule applies to all students, including students incarcerated in adult or juvenile facilities.

Plan for it during the transition IEPs in Step 8. Some families use supported decision-making arrangements so a parent can keep helping without court involvement. If a court has found your child incapacitated to make educational decisions, a guardian appointed in a protective proceeding under ORS Chapter 125 exercises the rights instead (OAR 581-015-2325(2)(a)) — and an adult student may ask that a surrogate be appointed to exercise their rights. After transfer, the district sends notices to your adult child — and, under federal rule, to you as well — but the decisions become your child's to make.
