The Oregon 504 Guide for Parents

A step-by-step guide to Section 504 plans in Oregon — what they are, how to get one, and how to protect it

Education Across the States · Charles Mayfield, Producer Current through November 5, 2026 — next scheduled review July 2027

How to use this guide

A 504 plan is a school district's written plan of accommodations for a student whose disability substantially limits a major life activity — learning, concentrating, seeing, hearing, walking, breathing, communicating, and many others. It comes from Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, a federal civil rights law, and from the U.S. Department of Education's regulations at 34 CFR Part 104.

Here is the Oregon difference that shapes this whole guide: most states leave 504 exactly where the federal government put it. Oregon adds a state layer. Oregon's rules give parents a formal dispute route that runs through the state — including the right to request a hearing before the Superintendent of Public Instruction over a 504 decision (OAR 581-015-2395) — and Oregon's abbreviated-school-day law expressly protects students with 504 plans. This guide covers the federal floor first, then the Oregon layer on top of it. Where a rule is federal, it says so; where Oregon adds something, it says that too.

If your child also may need specially designed instruction, start with our Oregon IEP Guide for Parents. The two guides cross-reference each other throughout.

Part 1 — The basics

Step 1: What Section 504 actually is

Section 504 is an anti-discrimination law. Its school regulations (34 CFR Part 104, Subpart D) do four things:

  1. They require districts to find and evaluate students who may have disabilities (child find, §104.32; evaluation, §104.35).
  2. They require a free appropriate public education — regular or special education and related aids and services designed to meet the student's individual needs as adequately as the needs of nondisabled students are met (§104.33).
  3. They require placement decisions made by a group of people who know the child, the evaluation data, and the placement options, in the least restrictive setting (§104.34, §104.35(c)).
  4. They require procedural safeguards — notice, a chance to examine records, an impartial hearing, and a review procedure (§104.36).

Section 504 covers students in districts that receive federal funds — which is every Oregon public school district. It also reaches preschool and adult education programs and, differently, private schools (Step 11).

What a 504 plan is not. It is not specialized instruction (that is an IEP). It is not a diagnosis certificate — a diagnosis alone does not entitle a student to a plan, and the absence of a diagnosis does not disqualify one. And it is not a favor the school grants when convenient: once a student qualifies, the accommodations are obligations.

Step 2: Who is covered

Section 504 protects a student who (34 CFR 104.3):

  • has a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities;
  • has a record of such an impairment; or
  • is regarded as having such an impairment.

Major life activities (federal, as broadened by the ADA Amendments Act of 2008) include caring for oneself, walking, seeing, hearing, speaking, breathing, learning, reading, concentrating, thinking, communicating, and working — and major bodily functions such as the immune, neurological, brain, respiratory, circulatory, and endocrine systems. "Substantially limits" is read broadly — it does not mean the student is failing, and mitigating measures (medication, devices, learned coping strategies) are generally disregarded: the question is the impairment's impact without them. Episodic conditions count when active.

Common 504 territory: ADHD, anxiety, depression, severe allergies, asthma, diabetes, dyslexia or other learning disorders that do not require specialized instruction, temporary injuries, and medical conditions managed during the school day.

Oregon specifics. Oregon's own rules adopt the federal definition for the state's 504 hearing system: a "student with a disability under Section 504" means a student who has a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities (OAR 581-015-2390(1) — the definitions rule for Oregon 504 hearings). And Oregon gives that definition somewhere to go: the state's 504 hearing rule (Step 10) is written in the federal law's own terms — identification, evaluation, provision of a free appropriate public education, and educational placement. When an Oregon 504 dispute lands, it lands in federal language with a state door to knock on.

Step 3: The evaluation — and the honest truth about Oregon timelines

The federal process. A district must evaluate any student it believes may need services because of a disability, before initial placement and before any later significant change in placement (§104.35(a)). Tests must be validated for their purpose, administered by trained personnel, and chosen to measure aptitude or achievement — not a sensory, manual, or speaking impairment the test would merely reflect. Placement decisions must draw on information from a variety of sources — aptitude and achievement tests, teacher recommendations, physical-condition records, social or cultural background, adaptive behavior — evaluated and documented by a group of persons knowledgeable about the child, the data, and the placement options (§104.35(b)–(c)). Districts must also reevaluate periodically (§104.35(d)).

Ask in writing. Write to the principal or the district's 504 coordinator: "I am requesting that my child be evaluated under Section 504." Describe what you observe. Districts must designate a 504 coordinator and publish grievance procedures (§104.7) — find your coordinator's name on the district website or by asking the front office; that name belongs in your notebook.

Oregon specifics — no state day-count exists. Say so plainly. Oregon's Division 15 rules regulate 504 in exactly one place — the hearing rules described in Step 10 (procedures at OAR 581-015-2395; definitions at OAR 581-015-2390). Oregon sets no state deadline for completing a 504 evaluation, and there is no federal day-count either: the federal rule requires evaluation before placement and reevaluation before a significant change in placement, judged by the reasonableness of the district's process. Anyone who gives you an Oregon 504 deadline in days is inventing it — including anyone's template letter. What you can enforce: your dated written request, the requirement of a varied-source evaluation by people who know your child, and the grievance and hearing routes in Step 10 if the district stalls, refuses, or rubber-stamps.

Oregon's Department of Education reaches 504 questions through its Civil Rights office — its Section 504 page (oregon.gov/ode → Students and Family → Equity → Civil Rights → Section 504) gives the contact ODE.CivilRightsSupport@ode.oregon.gov — and its Dispute Resolution page routes Section 504 disputes to those Civil Rights resources. Save both signposts.

Watch out for: an evaluation consisting of one teacher's impression, or a single screening score. Federal law requires decisions from a variety of sources, documented and carefully considered. If the "evaluation" was one meeting and a shrug, it did not meet the federal standard — and that is exactly the kind of record a 504 hearing (Step 10) is built on.

📥 Our Evaluation Request Letter (in the IEP guide's downloads) is written for an IDEA evaluation; use the same letter with "under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act" substituted, or ask for both evaluations at once — schools frequently must consider both.

Step 4: The 504 plan itself

What this means. Unlike the IEP, a 504 plan is not a federally prescribed document with a federally required contents list. No federal form exists. A sound plan nevertheless records, in writing: the impairment and the major life activity it limits; the evaluation data relied on; each accommodation and service, who provides it, and where; the placement decision and the knowledgeable group that made it; and the procedural-safeguards notice. If your district's plan is a checkbox sheet with three vague accommodations ("preferential seating," "extended time as needed"), press for specifics: how much extended time, on what, where, supervised by whom.

What to do.

  1. Get the plan in writing, with your signature line understood: your signature records attendance and receipt — write "attendance only" if the district treats it as agreement and you are not yet in agreement.
  2. Confirm every classroom teacher and relevant staff member actually receives the plan. A plan in a drawer accommodates no one.
  3. Keep your copy with the evaluation data. When implementation fails, the plan plus your dated notes are the record.

Step 5: IEP or 504 — which road?

  • Needs specially designed instruction — teaching adapted in content, methodology, or delivery because of a disability → IEP (IDEA; Oregon: ORS 343 / OAR 581-015).
  • Can learn with accommodations — access changes, not instruction changes → 504 plan.
  • Both laws must be considered. A child found ineligible for an IEP must still be considered under 504, and a child on a 504 plan whose needs grow must be evaluated under IDEA. Districts that treat "no IEP" as "no plan at all" are wrong under both laws.

The protections differ and the difference matters: an IEP carries a state complaint system, mediation, due process before an administrative law judge, and stay-put rights (see the IEP guide, Part 5). A 504 plan carries Oregon's state hearing right (Step 10 of this guide), the district grievance process, and the federal Office for Civil Rights — strong tools, but a different ladder, with no stay-put (Step 10). If a district offers a 504 plan as a consolation for an IEP it should have granted, that choice deserves a hard look and, often, an IDEA evaluation demand in writing.

Part 2 — Keeping the plan alive

Step 6: If the school says no

A refusal to evaluate or to find your child eligible must come with the procedural safeguards §104.36 guarantees: notice of the decision and its basis, access to the records the decision relied on, and your right to an impartial hearing and a review procedure. Ask for the refusal in writing with the data relied on named. Then work the ladder in Step 10 — in Oregon, the ladder starts inside the state (the 504 hearing request) and the district grievance office, not only in Washington, D.C.

Watch out for: the verbal no. "We've decided he doesn't qualify" at a hallway conversation is not a determination you can appeal. Written refusals create records; records create options.

Step 7: Annual reviews and reevaluation

Federal law requires periodic reevaluation (§104.35(d)) and reevaluation before a significant change in placement (§104.35(a)). It does not set a calendar. Good Oregon practice — and the standard to hold your district to in writing — is a review of the plan at least annually and reevaluation at least every three years (the IEP guide's rhythms), plus reevaluation immediately when: the accommodations stop working; the condition changes; a discipline removal looms (Step 8); or the student moves between school levels (elementary → middle → high school), where accommodations routinely fall off the truck.

Put review dates in the plan itself. A plan with no review date is a plan designed to be forgotten.

Step 8: Discipline under 504 — read this carefully, because a popular claim is wrong

First, the correction. You will read — in widely used national parent guides — that a student with a 504 plan must receive a "manifestation determination review" (MDR) before discipline, as IEP students do. That is not the law. The MDR is an IDEA protection (for IEP students, in Oregon: OAR 581-015-2420). Section 504's federal regulations contain no MDR requirement, and Oregon's 504 rules do not add one.

What 504 actually requires (federal). Before a district makes a significant change in placement — and the U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights (OCR) treats a disciplinary exclusion totaling more than 10 school days in a school year as such a change — the district must reevaluate the student: the knowledgeable group reconvenes, reviews current data, and determines whether the misconduct is linked to the disability and whether the placement and plan remain appropriate (34 CFR 104.35(a), (c); OCR interpretation of the 504 regulations). If the behavior is disability-linked, a removal built on it is a discriminatory placement change; if the plan itself was not implemented, that failure belongs in the reevaluation record as well.

The practical differences from an IEP that matter:

  • No 504 "stay-put": a disciplined 504 student does not automatically remain in the prior placement while you dispute the removal. Speed matters — file the grievance/hearing request fast (Step 10).
  • Drug and alcohol discipline is narrower than parents expect: federal law permits districts to discipline a student for current illegal drug or alcohol use to the same extent as nondisabled students, without the disability protections — but alcoholism and drug addiction themselves remain impairments that may qualify the student for a plan governing everything else.
  • Oregon's abbreviated-school-day law covers 504 students expressly (Step 9): repeated "come get your child early" is the informal version of a disciplinary removal, and Oregon law names it.

Step 9: Oregon's abbreviated-day law protects 504 students too

Oregon's 2023 statute (ORS 343.321–343.331) was written for students with disabilities — and its definition of that term expressly includes a student who has a disability under Section 504 and is eligible for a 504 Plan (ORS 343.321). ODE's implementation guidance confirms it: students with 504 plans are inside the abbreviated-day regime, with the 504 team holding the review meetings. The protections, in brief:

  • A district may not unilaterally place a student on an abbreviated school day program, and may not do it because of inadequate staffing.
  • An abbreviated program requires the informed written consent of the parent (or foster parent); without consent, the student is entitled to meaningful access to the same hours as the majority of students in the same grade.
  • With a program in place, the team must review it on the statute's schedule and whenever the parent requests; consent is revocable in writing at any time, and revocation obliges the district to restore full access.
  • ODE counts compliance: districts report every student with an IEP, a 504 plan, or an evaluation in progress placed on an abbreviated day program for more than 10 school days a year (ODE Abbreviated Day Collection, July 1–June 30 reporting period).

The full step-by-step is in our Oregon IEP Guide, Step 17 — the consent, review, and revocation mechanics are the same for 504 families. If your 504 student is on a shortened day tonight with no signed consent on file, that is the first letter to write.

Step 10: When you disagree — Oregon's 504 ladder

Work from the fastest remedy outward. Unlike IDEA disputes, 504 clocks are unforgiving: mark dates the day a decision lands.

Rung 1 — The knowledgeable group and the 504 coordinator. Most 504 failures are implementation failures. Write to the 504 coordinator (every district must designate one, §104.7) describing the plan term not delivered, with dates. Ask for a reconvened placement group and a written response.

Rung 2 — The district grievance procedure. Every district must adopt and publish a grievance procedure for disability discrimination complaints (§104.7). File in writing, keep the stamped copy, and note the district's own response deadline in its policy. ODE's Dispute Resolution page routes 504 complainants to ODE's Civil Rights resources — use them for guidance on what Oregon expects districts' procedures to contain.

Rung 3 — The Oregon 504 hearing (the state layer most states don't have). Oregon rule OAR 581-015-2395 provides: a parent or guardian who is dissatisfied with a district's action (or refusal to act) regarding their child's identification, evaluation, provision of a free appropriate public education, or educational placement under Section 504 may file a written request for a hearing with the State Superintendent of Public Instruction — the Oregon Department of Education. The hearing then runs under Oregon's IDEA hearing procedures (OAR 581-015-2340 through 581-015-2383), with two express exceptions (OAR 581-015-2395(4)) that you must plan around:

  • No stay-put. The stay-put protection that keeps a child in the current placement during IDEA proceedings does not apply to a 504 hearing (OAR 581-015-2395(4)). The placement the district chose is the placement while you fight — act fast, and consider asking the hearing officer for interim relief.
  • No free verbatim record. The IDEA hearing right to obtain a written or electronic verbatim record of the hearing at no cost also does not apply (OAR 581-015-2395(4)).

The district pays the costs of the hearing (OAR 581-015-2395(2)). File the request with the Oregon Department of Education — the Dispute Resolution Section can confirm the current routing: Oregon Department of Education, Office of Student Services — Dispute Resolution Section, 255 Capitol Street NE, Salem, OR 97310 · (503) 947-5797 · ode.disputeresolution@ode.oregon.gov.

Rung 4 — The U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights (OCR). A federal complaint of disability discrimination must generally be filed within 180 days of the act (a limited waiver exists for good cause). OCR investigates and can require corrective action; it does not award money damages. Filing with OCR does not require exhausting Rungs 1–3 first — if discrimination (not just disagreement) is the claim, OCR can be Rung 1. (Federal; the 180-day rule is OCR's own.)

Rung 5 — Court. Section 504 claims may be brought in court. Exhaustion requirements, damages rules, and deadlines are lawyer questions; for a case with real stakes, an Oregon education or civil-rights attorney earns their fee at this rung.

Watch out for: assuming IDEA timelines protect a 504 filing. They don't. The two-year IDEA window in the IEP guide belongs to IDEA. OCR's window is 180 days. Oregon's 504 hearing rule sets no filing deadline in the rule itself — do not read silence as safety; file as if the shortest clock in this step applies.

Step 11: Private school and 504

Section 504 covers students in private schools only in narrow ways: if a district places or refers a student to a private program, the 504 FAPE obligations travel with the placement (§104.33(c)); if parents enroll privately on their own, the child has no individual right to services from the district under 504 — the district's duty is its Child Find obligation, and any services come through the IDEA's proportionate-share framework, not a 504 plan. A private school that receives federal funds has its own, separate 504 obligations to its students (§104.39) — enforced through OCR, not through the district.

Step 12: 504 after high school — the thirty-second version

Section 504 (and the ADA) follow your student to college; the IEP does not. The model flips: the student must self-identify to the college's disability office, provide documentation, and request accommodations — colleges need not modify essential program requirements, and there is no college "504 plan" meeting with parents. Use the last high school 504 review to assemble the documentation file: the plan, the evaluation data behind it, and a summary of which accommodations actually worked. That file is the bridge.

Oregon 504 facts worth memorizing

Question Answer Source
Is there an Oregon 504 evaluation deadline? No — neither state nor federal law sets a day-count; the standard is evaluation before placement, from a variety of sources 34 CFR 104.35; OAR 581-015 (no 504 evaluation timeline set)
Must a 504 plan be written? No federal form exists — but a written plan is the only enforceable kind; insist on one 34 CFR 104.33–104.35
Is there a manifestation determination under 504? No. The rule is reevaluation before a significant change in placement; >10 cumulative exclusion days triggers it (OCR) 34 CFR 104.35(a); OCR interpretation
Can I get a state hearing on a 504 decision? Yes — Oregon-specific. Written request to the State Superintendent; IDEA hearing procedures apply, minus stay-put and the free verbatim record; district pays costs OAR 581-015-2395; definitions at OAR 581-015-2390
Does stay-put protect my child during a 504 dispute? No — plan around it; act fast OAR 581-015-2395(4)
OCR complaint deadline 180 days from the act (federal) OCR case-processing rule
Abbreviated school days Consent required; 504 students expressly covered; >10 days/year reported to ODE ORS 343.322–343.331
