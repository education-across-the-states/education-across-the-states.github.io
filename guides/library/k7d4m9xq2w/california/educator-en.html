The California Educator's Playbook: IEPs and 504 Plans

A practitioner guide to doing special education right in California — by workflow, with the state's actual timelines and forms discipline

Education Across the States · Charles Mayfield, Producer Current through November 5, 2026 — next scheduled review July 2027

Who this is for and how it is organized

You are a case manager, school psychologist, speech-language pathologist, special education teacher, 504 coordinator, counselor, or site administrator in a California school. Your work is organized by what you do next, so this playbook is too: identify → evaluate → write → implement → monitor → meet. IEP and Section 504 run side by side through every stage, with the differences flagged where they bite.

Every timeline in this playbook is stated as California law actually sets it. Where the rule is federal, it is labeled federal. Where a practice standard is professional judgment rather than law, it is labeled practice.

Stage 1 — Identify: Child Find and the referral

The duty. Your district must identify, locate, and evaluate children who may have disabilities (federal Child Find; California Education Code Part 30). Child Find does not wait for a parent who knows the vocabulary. A student who is failing, a student whose behavior is escalating, a student with a chronic health condition and mounting absences — each is a Child Find referral waiting to be written.

Practice standards that keep you compliant:

  • Intervention tiers are not a gate. MTSS/RTI data is valuable input to an evaluation; it is not a lawful precondition. If a parent requests an evaluation in writing, or a disability is suspected, the referral goes forward while interventions continue. A referral that sits in a student-study-team loop while a parent's written request ages is the single most common Child Find failure pattern.
  • Referrals are dated, always. California's assessment-plan clock runs from the district's receipt of the referral. A referral that lives in an email thread without a logged receipt date has already created a compliance problem. Log receipt the day it arrives, in the system of record.
  • 504 runs on the same instinct. Section 504 has its own Child Find duty (34 CFR 104.32). A student who doesn't fit an IDEA category may still need a 504 evaluation. Train staff to refer to both doors.
  • Who can refer: teachers, parents, physicians, agencies, the student. Treat every referral as the start of a legal timeline, not a conversation.

Stage 2 — Evaluate: the assessment plan and the 60-day clock

California's mechanics (EC §56321, §56344) — learn them exactly:

  1. Within 15 days of the referral (not counting days between regular school sessions/terms or vacation days beyond five schooldays), the district must give the parent a proposed assessment plan in writing. A referral received within 10 days of the end of the school year pushes the plan to within 10 days after the next year starts.
  2. The parent has at least 15 days from receipt of the plan to decide (EC §56321(c)(4)). Build your scheduling around that floor, not around the hope of a fast signature.
  3. Consent to assess is not consent to place (EC §56321(d)) — say this to families explicitly; it builds the trust the rest of the process needs. Assessment may begin immediately on consent.
  4. From the district's receipt of written consent: assessment completed and IEP developed within 60 days total (EC §56344(a)). One clock — not 60 to evaluate plus 30 to meet. If consent arrives within 30 days of the end of the school year, the IEP is due within 30 days after the next school year begins. Count the break exclusions in writing, in the file, every time.

Assessment quality standards:

  • All areas of suspected disability (federal 34 CFR 300.304; EC §56320). The plan must map to the referral concerns — if the referral says behavior and the plan says academics only, the file contradicts itself.
  • Assess in the student's native language or mode of communication, with validated instruments, administered by qualified personnel, and never a single measure as the sole criterion.
  • Parent copies before the meeting. Families are entitled to the assessment reports the team will rely on. Practically: a report handed across the table at the IEP meeting is a procedural complaint in embryo. Send reports ahead, with a named contact for questions.
  • Independent evaluations. If the family disagrees with your assessment, they may request an Independent Educational Evaluation at public expense. The district must fund it or file due process to defend its assessment — without unnecessary delay (34 CFR 300.502). There is no day count in the law; there is also no tolerance for drift. Route IEE requests to administration the day they arrive, with the date stamped. If an independent evaluation arrives from the family, the team must consider it — document the consideration in the IEP notes, not just the receipt.
  • Reevaluation consent. For reevaluations, document the reasonable measures taken to obtain consent; federal law permits proceeding without consent if the parent fails to respond after those documented measures (as California's safeguards notice states). "We sent one form" is not documented reasonable measures.

Stage 3 — Write: documents that say what they mean

The IEP (EC §56345; federal content list at 34 CFR 300.320)

A defensible California IEP contains, every time:

  • Present levels with measurable baselines tied to data — the numbers the goals will be measured against.
  • Measurable annual goals with the measurement method named. If a goal cannot be scored, it cannot be progress-monitored, and it will not survive scrutiny.
  • Services with frequency, duration, location, and start date. "Speech 2×/month, 30 min, small group" is a commitment. "Speech as needed" is a finding waiting to happen.
  • An LRE explanation for any time outside general education: what supplementary aids and services were considered, and why they were insufficient.
  • State assessment participation and accommodations, decided by the team, not by default settings.
  • Transition planning on California's earlier trigger: the individualized transition plan begins when the student starts their high school experience, or not later than the first IEP in effect at age 16 — whichever comes first (EC §§56043(g)(1), 56345(a)(8), as amended by AB 438, effective July 1, 2025). Annual updates; measurable postsecondary goals grounded in age-appropriate transition assessment; if the team defers to 16 despite high school entry, the file should state the basis. Meeting notices for transition meetings must state the purpose and that the student (and, with consent, outside agencies) will be invited (EC §§56043(f), 56341.5(e)).

Prior written notice — the discipline that saves cases

PWN (federal 34 CFR 300.503; California notice provisions cited in the state's safeguards notice) is required whenever the district proposes or refuses to initiate or change identification, evaluation, placement, or the provision of FAPE. California's safeguards notice lists the required contents: what action is proposed/refused, why, the data relied on, the other options considered and why they were rejected, and where parents can get help understanding their rights.

Practice: write the PWN as if the hearing officer is the reader — because in any later dispute, they are. "Per parent request, considered and rejected" without the why and the data is the pattern that loses procedural cases. Issue PWN even when the parent attended and agreed verbally; the written record is the compliance.

The 504 plan

No federal format exists — which transfers the burden to your writing. A workmanlike 504 plan states the impairment, the major life activity limited, each accommodation with provider/frequency/trigger, who informs each teacher (including substitutes), health procedures where relevant, and a review date. Evaluate with a knowledgeable group, from multiple sources, and document both (34 CFR 104.35). Reevaluate periodically and before any significant change in placement — see Stage 5 for what that means in discipline.

Stage 4 — Implement: the document is the job

  • Implement as written, from the start date. Federal law expects implementation as soon as possible after the meeting. A two-week "ramp-up" that exists only in practice is non-implementation.
  • Every teacher informed. Federal law requires the IEP to be accessible to each of the student's teachers and providers, and each must be informed of their specific responsibilities — accommodations included. For 504 plans, the same discipline is the district's only real implementation mechanism. Document the notification (who, when, how) for both.
  • Log services contemporaneously. Minutes delivered, dated, initialed, at the time of service. A service log reconstructed in March for a December complaint reads as what it is. In California's dispute volume (Stage 6), the service log is the most-requested and most-damning-or-exonerating document in the file.
  • Caseload reality check. California law caps a resource specialist's caseload at 28 pupils (EC §56362) — verify your assignment against it, and raise overages in writing to administration. The cap is the educator's protection as much as the student's.

Stage 5 — Monitor: progress, reviews, and your data's second life

  • Progress reports go to families on the schedule the IEP itself states. Report against the goal's own measurement method, with data. "Progressing" without numbers teaches families to distrust every report that follows.
  • Annual review at least every 12 months; reevaluation at least every three years unless parent and district agree otherwise in writing (federal). Diarize both from the consent/eligibility dates — not from memory.
  • Your entries feed state monitoring. California's Annual Determination process draws timeline compliance from CALPADS — including Report 16.21's data on late annual plan reviews and late triennial reevaluations (CDE CALPADS Update Flash #314). A missed date in your caseload is not a private slip; it aggregates into your LEA's determination letter. Enter plan dates accurately and on time; audit your own caseload's annuals and triennials against the system quarterly.
  • 504 reviews: no California or federal day count exists — which means the review date on the plan itself is the commitment. Honor it, and reevaluate before any significant change in placement, including exclusionary discipline: a removal beyond 10 consecutive school days, or a pattern of removals totaling more than 10 school days, is treated by the U.S. Office for Civil Rights as a significant change in placement requiring reevaluation first. Know this before the suspension packet crosses your desk — 504 discipline has no manifestation determination review (that is IDEA), and running the wrong procedure is its own violation.

Stage 6 — Meet: running a meeting that holds up

Before. Notice early enough for the family to attend; confirm interpreter needs; circulate assessment reports and, where your practice uses them, clearly-marked drafts — drafts are working papers, and the meeting must be able to change them. A draft treated as final is predetermination, the procedural error families' advocates look for first.

Membership. Required members present or properly excused under the federal rules (parent agreement in writing for excusals where the member's area is not discussed; written input where it is). The district representative at the table must be able to commit resources — a representative who must "check with the office" on every service question undermines the meeting's legal function.

Family rights in the room — plan around them, don't be surprised by them:

  • A parent may audio-record on 24 hours' notice (EC §56341.1(g)(1)). If the district records, it gives the same notice — and if the parent does not consent to the district recording, the meeting shall not be recorded. Assume you are on the record at every meeting; it improves meetings.
  • Parents may bring advocates or attorneys without permission. Welcome them; the goal is a document everyone can implement.
  • Parents may take the IEP home unsigned. Offer it. Consent given after reflection is sturdier than consent extracted at the table.

If it goes sideways. California's system expects most conflict to resolve before a hearing: facilitated meetings and ADR through your SELPA, mediation-only requests to OAH, state complaints to CDE's Complaint Resolution Unit (60-day investigations), and due process before OAH administrative law judges. The volume is not trivial — OAH's own quarterly report records 3,762 special education cases filed in the first three quarters of FY2025-26 alone, the overwhelming majority filed on behalf of students, with 2,009 mediations held in the same period. Mediation settles most of what it touches; the meetings that end in agreements there are the ones where the paper was clean.

What makes an IEP defensible at OAH (practice standards, stated plainly):

  1. Contemporaneous records — logs, data sheets, and notes written when the work happened.
  2. Goals that were measured the way the IEP said they would be, with the data in the file.
  3. PWNs that explain — action, reason, data, alternatives — every time the district said no to anything.
  4. Timelines met or break-exclusions documented in the file, in days, from the trigger dates.
  5. Parent input visibly considered — in the notes, in the present levels, in the options considered and rejected. A file that shows the family was heard, even where the team disagreed, is the file that reads as a team.
