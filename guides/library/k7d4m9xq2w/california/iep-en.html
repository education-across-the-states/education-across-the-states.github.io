The California IEP Guide for Parents

A step-by-step guide to special education in California — written for the parent doing this at 11 PM

Education Across the States · Charles Mayfield, Producer Current through November 5, 2026 — next scheduled review July 2027

How to use this guide

An IEP — an Individualized Education Program — is the written plan that spells out what special education and related services a school district must provide to a child with a disability. It is a legal document, not a favor. When a district agrees to something in an IEP, it has to do it.

This guide walks the whole road in order: how a child gets evaluated, how the IEP gets built, how you stay in charge of it year after year, and what to do when something goes wrong. California has its own timelines and its own offices, and they are often better for parents than the federal defaults you will read about in national guides. Where California's rule differs from the federal rule, this guide says so. Where a rule is federal, it says that too.

You do not need to read it cover to cover. If you are at a specific step tonight, go straight to that step.

Terms used throughout: District means your local school district (in California law, the "local educational agency," or LEA — charter schools are usually their own LEA for special education). SELPA means your Special Education Local Plan Area — California organizes special education through regional SELPAs, and your district belongs to one. FAPE means a free appropriate public education — the legal standard everything in this guide is measured against.

Part 1 — The basics

What an IEP is

Federal law — the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) — gives every eligible child with a disability the right to a free appropriate public education in the least restrictive environment. California law (Education Code Part 30, starting at §56000) builds on the federal floor and, in several places, gives families more: faster timelines, faster records, earlier transition planning. This guide covers both layers and labels each one.

Who is covered

California serves children with disabilities from age 3 through age 21. For children from birth to age 3, early intervention runs through California's Early Start program (IDEA Part C), usually through a regional center — and Family Resource Centers, described in Part 4, exist to help those families. If your child is turning 3, the transition from Early Start to a school district IEP happens at the third birthday, and planning for it should start well before.

IEP or 504?

A child who needs specialized instruction because of a disability needs an IEP. A child who can learn with accommodations — changes to how they access learning, not what they are taught — may be served by a Section 504 plan instead. The two laws are different, the documents are different, and the dispute systems are different. Our companion California 504 Guide for Parents covers that road. One rule of thumb: if the school is evaluating your child, it should be considering both.

The school has a duty to find your child

Districts cannot wait for parents to figure this out. Federal and California law impose a Child Find duty: districts must identify, locate, and evaluate children who may have disabilities and need special education — including children who are passing their classes, children in private school, and children whose difficulties show up as behavior rather than grades. If you suspect a disability, you do not have to prove it before asking for an evaluation. Suspicion is the trigger. Step 1 starts there.

Part 2 — Getting evaluated

Step 1: Ask for an evaluation — in writing

What this means. Nothing in California special education starts until someone refers a child for assessment. A teacher can refer. So can you, at any time, for any child you reasonably suspect has a disability affecting their education.

What to do.

  1. Write a short letter or email to your child's teacher, principal, or the district's special education director. Say: "I am requesting that my child be evaluated (assessed) for special education eligibility under all areas of suspected disability." Name what you are seeing — reading, speech, attention, behavior, motor skills, social development — in plain words.
  2. Date it. Keep a copy. Email creates its own paper trail; if you hand-deliver, ask for a date-stamped copy.
  3. Do not accept "let's wait and see" or "we have to finish intervention tiers first" as a substitute for an evaluation. Intervention programs can run alongside an evaluation; they are not a legal waiting room in front of one.

California specifics. Your written request starts a clock the district cannot pause: within 15 days of receiving your referral, the district must give you a proposed assessment plan in writing (Education Code §56321(a)). How that plan works is Step 2.

Watch out for: a verbal "we'll look into it" with no assessment plan. If 15 days pass without a written plan, write again, reference your first request and its date, and keep both copies — that paper trail matters in Part 5.

Step 2: The assessment plan — your 15 days, and the district's

What this means. California does not let a district simply start testing. It must first send you an assessment plan: a written description of the areas to be assessed (for example: academics, speech and language, psychological processing, motor, health), who will assess them, and what kinds of tests or observations will be used. Nothing is assessed until you consent to the plan.

What to do.

  1. Read the plan against your concerns. Every area you named in Step 1 that the plan leaves out is a question to ask in writing: "Please add assessment in [area], because [what you observe]." A district must assess in all areas of suspected disability (federal rule) — a plan that only covers the one area the school noticed is incomplete.
  2. Check that the plan provides for assessment in your child's native language or mode of communication, and that it does not rely on any single test to decide eligibility.
  3. Sign and return it if it covers the right areas — or sign with your additions noted and keep a copy of what you actually signed.

California specifics. You have at least 15 days from receiving the plan to decide (EC §56321(c)(4)) — the district cannot pressure you to sign on the spot. The 15-day referral clock does not count days between regular school sessions or terms, or school vacation days beyond five schooldays, unless you agree in writing to an extension (EC §56321(a)). If a referral lands in the last 10 days of the school year, the plan is due within 10 days after the next school year starts.

Consent to assessment is not consent to placement. Signing the assessment plan gives permission to evaluate only. It does not enroll your child in special education, change their classes, or commit you to anything else (EC §56321(d)). The assessment may begin as soon as you consent.

Watch out for: signing a plan that assesses only academics when your concern is behavior or speech. The eligibility decision is only as good as the areas assessed — gaps here become "the evaluation didn't find anything" later.

Step 3: The evaluation — and California's single 60-day clock

What this means. Assessors observe your child, review records, talk to teachers, and test in the areas on the plan. Then the team — including you — meets to decide eligibility and, if your child is eligible, to build the IEP.

Here is the California difference most national guides get wrong. Federally, the familiar pattern is "60 days to evaluate, then 30 more days to hold the IEP meeting." California does not split it. Under EC §56344(a), the assessment must be completed and an IEP developed within a total of 60 days from the day the district receives your written consent to the assessment plan. One clock, one deadline, evaluation and IEP inside it. (The same school-break exclusions apply; if consent lands in the last 30 days of the school year, the IEP is due within 30 days after the next school year begins.)

What to do.

  1. Count 60 days from the date the district received your signed plan. Put it on your calendar.
  2. Ask for copies of the assessment reports before the IEP meeting so you can read them at your kitchen table, not across a conference table. You are entitled to copies of the reports the team will rely on.
  3. Bring your own information: report cards, work samples, private evaluations, medical letters, and your own written observations. The team must consider information you provide.
  4. If you disagree with the district's assessment when it is done, read Step 4 before the meeting ends.

California specifics. The state's own Notice of Procedural Safeguards puts it in one sentence: the assessment must be completed and an IEP developed within 60 days of your consent. If day 60 passes with no IEP meeting scheduled, write to the district citing EC §56344 and your consent date — that letter, kept, is Exhibit A if you ever need Part 5.

Watch out for: a meeting scheduled at day 59 with reports handed to you at the door. Ask to reschedule within the window with reports in hand ahead of time — or state on the record that you are proceeding without adequate time to review, and note it in the meeting notes.

Step 4: If you disagree with the evaluation — the independent evaluation (IEE)

What this means. If the district's evaluation gets your child wrong — wrong tests, wrong conclusions, areas missed — you have the right to an Independent Educational Evaluation by a qualified examiner who does not work for the district, at public expense: the district pays.

What to do.

  1. Say so, in writing, and say exactly which evaluation you disagree with. (Our IEE request letter does this.) You do not have to explain why in detail.
  2. Ask the district for its IEE criteria and information about where to obtain an IEE — the district must provide that information on request.
  3. The district then has exactly two lawful options: fund the IEE, or file for a due process hearing to prove its own evaluation was appropriate. There is no third option of ignoring you.

The rule, stated exactly. Federal law (34 CFR 300.502) requires the district to act "without unnecessary delay." There is no number of days — not five, not fifteen. Some national template letters invent a deadline here; do not rely on one, and do not let a district hide behind one either. What the law forbids is unnecessary delay: if weeks pass with no funded IEE and no hearing filed, that is the violation, and your dated request letter is the proof.

You may obtain one IEE at public expense each time the district conducts an evaluation you disagree with. You may also pay for your own independent evaluation at any time; either way, the IEP team must consider independent assessment results, and an independent assessor is entitled to the same classroom-observation access the district's own assessors had — including observing a proposed new placement before it happens.

Watch out for: "We'll just re-test." A second district evaluation is not an IEE. And a district that says it will fund an IEE but applies criteria so narrow (fee caps, approved lists) that no qualified examiner qualifies — that is a dispute for Part 5.

Step 5: Eligibility — the team's decision, with you on the team

What this means. The IEP team — which includes you — reviews the assessment results and decides two things: (1) does your child meet one of California's eligibility categories (state regulations list the categories, such as specific learning disability, speech or language impairment, autism, other health impairment, and others), and (2) because of the disability, does your child need special education and related services?

What to do.

  1. Ask for the reasoning in writing if your child is found ineligible: which category was considered, what data ruled it out. A district that refuses or ends eligibility must give you prior written notice — a written explanation of what it is refusing, why, what data it relied on, and what other options were considered (federal rule, 34 CFR 300.503; California's notice provisions are in the Education Code).
  2. If the team says "not eligible for an IEP," ask the 504 question in the same meeting: "Will the school evaluate for a Section 504 plan?" Many children who do not need specialized instruction still qualify for accommodations. See the companion 504 guide.
  3. If you disagree with an ineligibility decision, your options are the IEE (Step 4) and the dispute ladder (Part 5).

Watch out for: "Their grades are fine, so they don't qualify." Grades are one data point. Eligibility turns on disability and educational need — which includes functional, social, behavioral, and progress measures, not report cards alone.

Part 3 — Developing the IEP

Step 6: The IEP meeting — your seat at the table

What this means. If your child is eligible, the IEP is written by a team: you, at least one of your child's general education teachers, at least one special education teacher or provider, a district representative who can commit resources, someone who can explain the evaluation results, and — when transition is on the table — your child. Others with knowledge of your child can be invited by you or the district.

Your rights at the table (know these cold):

  • A meeting at a mutually agreed time and place, with enough notice for you to attend. If you cannot attend in person, the district must offer other ways to participate, including phone or video conference (federal rules).
  • An interpreter if you need one, at no cost to you.
  • Bring someone with you — a spouse, a friend, an advocate, an attorney. You do not need permission.
  • You may audio-record the meeting. California law (EC §56341.1(g)(1)) lets a parent record IEP team meetings with an electronic device if you give the district at least 24 hours' notice of your intent. The same rule binds the district: it may record with 24 hours' notice to you, and if you do not consent to the district recording, the meeting shall not be recorded. Recording removes every later argument about what was said. Use it.
  • Take the draft home. You do not have to sign agreement in the room. It is standard, and wise, to take the document, read it overnight, and return your signature page after.

California specifics. For a child found eligible, federal law expects the initial IEP meeting within 30 days of the eligibility determination (34 CFR 300.323(c)(1)) — but in California this is almost always inside the Step 3 clock anyway, because the 60 days from consent covers assessment and IEP together.

Watch out for: the completed IEP presented as a done deal — "we already met and drafted this, just sign." Drafts are legal; predetermination is not. The team, with you in it, must be able to change the document. If every page is final before discussion starts, say so on your recording and in writing afterward.

Step 7: What the IEP must contain

Under California law (EC §56345) and federal law, the IEP must include:

  1. Present levels — where your child is right now, academically and functionally, in measurable terms, including how the disability affects involvement and progress in the general curriculum.
  2. Measurable annual goals — what your child will achieve in a year, and how progress will be measured and reported to you.
  3. Special education and related services — the specific services (speech, occupational therapy, specialized academic instruction, counseling, transportation, and others), with how often, how long, where, and starting when. Vague language ("as needed," "consultation available") is not a service commitment.
  4. Program modifications and supports for school staff.
  5. The extent your child will not participate with nondisabled children in general education and activities — California and federal law both start from the least restrictive environment: your child learns alongside nondisabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate, and removal needs justification in the document.
  6. Assessment accommodations — how your child participates in state and district testing, or why an alternate assessment is appropriate.
  7. Transition planning — see Step 8; California starts earlier than most states.
  8. Progress reporting — you must be told how and how often you will receive reports on your child's progress toward the goals, at least as often as parents of nondisabled children get report cards (federal rule).

What to do. Read the services page like a contract — because it is one. Every service your child was promised in the meeting should appear with minutes/frequency. If it was said in the room and is not in the document, it does not exist.

Step 8: Transition planning — California starts at high school, not 16

What this means. Transition planning is the IEP's bridge to adult life: measurable goals for education/training, employment, and (where appropriate) independent living after high school, plus the services and courses that lead there.

California specifics — another place California beats the federal default. Federal law requires transition planning in the first IEP in effect at age 16. California changed its law (Assembly Bill 438, effective July 1, 2025): the individualized transition plan must begin when your child starts their high school experience, or not later than the first IEP in effect at age 16 — whichever comes first (EC §§56043(g)(1), 56345(a)(8)). The transition plan is updated annually. If the team proposes waiting until 16 even though your child has started high school, the team is encouraged to state its basis — ask for that basis in the meeting, on the record.

When a meeting will address transition, the meeting notice must say so and must state that your child will be invited, and that other agencies (such as the Department of Rehabilitation or the regional center) may be invited with consent (EC §§56043(f), 56341.5(e)). Your child's presence and voice are not decoration — postsecondary goals must be built on your child's own strengths, preferences, and interests.

Watch out for: a transition plan whose "goals" are generic ("will explore careers"). Measurable postsecondary goals, tied to assessments of your child's interests and abilities, are the legal standard.

Step 9: Signing — consent, and what it does and doesn't do

What this means. The IEP takes effect with your consent. Understand three different consents, because California and federal law treat them differently:

  • Consent to assess (Step 2) — permission to evaluate. Never consent to services or placement.
  • Consent to initial services. Federal law is strict here: if you refuse or do not respond to a request for consent to initial special education services, the district may not use due process to override you (34 CFR 300.300(b)). No IEP services begin without your yes.
  • Consent to the annual IEP. Read before you sign. If you cannot agree with part of what is proposed, say which parts, in writing, and use the ladder in Part 5 — do not let a disagreement about one service stall the parts everyone agrees on, and do not sign "in attendance" thinking it means agreement. If a signature line confuses attendance with consent, write next to your signature what you mean: "Signed to show attendance only — consent decision to follow in writing."

Watch out for: services starting late because "the paperwork wasn't final." Once you consent, the IEP must be implemented as soon as possible following the meeting (federal rule). A start date belongs in the document; hold the district to it.

Part 4 — Staying involved

Step 10: The IEP year — monitoring, annual review, reevaluation

What this means. An IEP is not a one-time event. Progress reports come to you on the schedule written in the IEP. The team must review the IEP at least once every 12 months (the annual review), and your child must be reevaluated at least once every three years unless you and the district agree in writing that a reevaluation is unnecessary (federal rules, 34 CFR 300.303, 300.324(b)). You can request an IEP meeting at any time — put the request in writing and keep the date.

What to do.

  1. Keep an IEP binder or folder: every IEP, every assessment, every progress report, every email. One folder, chronological.
  2. Compare each progress report against the goal's own measurement method. "Making progress" without data is an opinion; the IEP promised measurement.
  3. Watch the triennial (three-year reevaluation) date and the annual review date the way you watch the 60-day clock. Both are your child's protection against a stale plan.

California specifics — records in five business days. California gives you a records right far stronger than the federal default: you may examine all of your child's school records and receive copies within five business days of your oral or written request (EC §56504) — and the district must comply without unnecessary delay, and in any case before any IEP meeting, hearing, or resolution session about your child. Copies may be charged at no more than the actual cost of reproduction, and if the cost would effectively prevent you from exercising the right, copies must be free. (For comparison, the federal FERPA default allows schools up to 45 days. In California, it is five business days.)

Watch out for: the annual review held without you because "it was just routine." There is no routine exception. You are a required member of the team, and a meeting without proper notice and a genuine opportunity to participate is a procedural violation — Part 5 territory.

Step 11: You are not alone — California's parent centers

California funds a support structure most parents never hear about. Use it — it exists for you, and it is free.

  • Family Empowerment Centers (FECs). Created by state law in 2001 (EC §§56400–56415), FECs are parent-led nonprofits that serve families of children and young adults with disabilities ages 3 through 22 in their communities: training, peer support, information, and help understanding and navigating exactly the process in this guide. CDE's California Parent Organizations page lists every FEC by the counties it serves.
  • Parent Training and Information Centers (PTICs) — federally funded parent centers listed on the same CDE page, offering training and individual assistance.
  • Community Parent Resource Centers — parent centers serving targeted communities, also listed by CDE.
  • Family Resource Centers — for families in Early Start (birth to 3), support during the Part C years and the transition to preschool services.
  • Your SELPA's Community Advisory Committee (CAC). Every SELPA has one, made up substantially of parents of students with disabilities. The CAC reviews the SELPA's local plan, advises the SELPA, and runs parent trainings. It is both a resource and a room where patterns get noticed — a problem three families raise is no longer one family's problem.
  • CDE's Procedural Safeguards Referral Service: 800-926-0648 — the state line for questions about the safeguards notice and dispute options.

Find your centers: CDE's California Parent Organizations page (cde.ca.gov — search "California Parent Organizations").

Part 5 — Rights and disputes

Disagreements are normal in special education. The system has a ladder of options, from a phone call to a hearing, and you are allowed to start low and climb. Going up the ladder does not require finishing the rung below — but the low rungs are faster, cheaper, and often work. Every option below is described as California actually runs it.

Step 12: The first moves — put it in writing, call the meeting

What to do.

  1. Write to the district describing the problem and the fix you want. Ask for prior written notice if the district is refusing something: the district must explain in writing what it refuses, why, the data it relied on, and the options it considered (34 CFR 300.503).
  2. Request an IEP meeting to address the problem. Many disputes are IEP problems wearing a disguise — the wrong service minutes, an unimplemented accommodation, a stale goal — and the meeting is where the document can be fixed.
  3. Request your records (five business days, EC §56504) before any meeting or filing. Decide from documents, not memories.

Step 13: Mediation and alternative dispute resolution (ADR)

What this means. California offers two related off-ramps before a hearing:

  • Mediation through the Office of Administrative Hearings (OAH): a trained, impartial mediator helps you and the district reach a written, legally binding agreement. You may request mediation only — without filing for a due process hearing at all. Mediation is voluntary, confidential, and free to you.
  • Alternative dispute resolution (ADR) through your SELPA: many California SELPAs run local ADR programs — facilitated IEP meetings, resolution sessions with a neutral facilitator, and local mediation — designed to resolve disagreements inside the community before they become cases. Ask your SELPA what it offers; California's procedural safeguards notice expressly points families to ADR as the less adversarial route.

When to use it. When the relationship still works but the decision doesn't. A mediated agreement is enforceable — it is a contract-like written agreement, not a handshake.

Step 14: The state complaint — CDE investigates, within 60 days

What this means. If the district violated special education law — missed a timeline, failed to implement an IEP, skipped a required procedure — you (or anyone) may file a written compliance complaint with the California Department of Education, and CDE must investigate and issue a written decision.

What to do.

  1. Write a signed complaint stating: the violation, the facts behind it, your contact information, and your child's information. The violation must have occurred not more than one year before CDE receives the complaint.
  2. Send a copy to the district at the same time you file with CDE — this is required.
  3. File with: California Department of Education, Special Education Division — Complaint Resolution Unit, 1430 N Street, Suite 2401, Sacramento, CA 95814-5901. Email: speceducation@cde.ca.gov · Fax: 916-327-3704. CDE publishes model complaint forms on its Special Education Complaint Process page.
  4. CDE issues its investigation report within 60 calendar days of receiving the complete complaint, extendable only for exceptional circumstances. If CDE finds a violation, it can order corrective action — including, where services were denied, compensatory services.

California specifics. Since July 1, 2020, special education compliance complaints have their own regulations — 5 CCR §§3200–3205 — and are not handled under the Uniform Complaint Procedures (UCP). (California's current safeguards notice still cites the older UCP regulation in places; the complaint itself goes to the Complaint Resolution Unit above either way.) UCP still matters for discrimination complaints — see the 504 guide.

A state complaint is free, needs no lawyer, and — important — can be filed by any individual or organization, not only parents. Teachers and advocates use this route too.

Watch out for: the one-year window. It runs from the violation, not from when you learned the rule. When in doubt, file.

Step 15: Due process — the hearing

What this means. Due process is the formal legal proceeding: you file a complaint with the Office of Administrative Hearings (OAH), Special Education Division, an administrative law judge hears evidence, and the judge issues a binding decision. Parents and districts can both file. This is the route for disagreements about identification, evaluation, placement, or FAPE itself — the merits, not just the procedures.

Contact: OAH Special Education Division, 2349 Gateway Oaks Drive, Suite 200, Sacramento, CA 95833-4231 · Phone (916) 263-0880 · Fax 916-263-0890. Filings are submitted through OAH's Secure e-File Transfer (SFT) system; OAH publishes a combined Request for Due Process Hearing and Mediation form (in English, Spanish, and other languages). The party who files must serve a copy on the other party — the form process includes proof of service.

The timeline, as it actually runs:

  1. Filing window: a due process request must allege a violation that occurred not more than two years before you knew or should have known about it (20 USC 1415; EC §56505).
  2. Resolution meeting: within 15 days of the district receiving a parent's due process complaint, the district must convene a resolution session with you and the relevant team members — unless both sides agree in writing to waive it or to use mediation instead.
  3. Resolution period: 30 days from the district's receipt of the complaint to resolve it. If you reach agreement, get it in writing — a signed resolution agreement is legally binding.
  4. Hearing and decision: if the 30 days pass without resolution, the hearing proceeds and the judge's decision is due 45 days from the end of the resolution period (extensions possible at either party's request).
  5. Stay put: while a due process case (and any appeal) is pending, your child generally remains in their current educational placement unless you and the district agree otherwise — the federal "stay-put" rule (20 USC 1415(j)). File before a disputed change takes effect whenever you can.

Practical notes. You may represent yourself; many parents do, and many hire a special education attorney or advocate for this stage. Judges decide on the record — your binder from Step 10, your recordings from Step 6, and your dated letters from Steps 1–4 are the evidence. Filing for due process does not cancel the other rungs: mediation remains available after filing, and most California cases settle through it.

Discipline cases are fast-tracked: a due process request about discipline (Step 16) is expedited — the hearing must occur within 20 school days of the request.

Step 16: Discipline — the protections that travel with the IEP

What this means. A child with an IEP can be disciplined — but the law builds a floor under the process, because removal from school is also removal from FAPE.

  • Short removals. A district may remove a child for up to 10 consecutive school days for a code violation, to the extent the same removal applies to nondisabled students. Additional removals of up to 10 days each for separate incidents are allowed during the school year, so long as they do not add up to a change of placement.
  • After 10 cumulative days in a school year, the district must provide services during further removals so your child continues to participate in the general curriculum and progress toward IEP goals, even in another setting.
  • The manifestation determination. When a removal becomes a disciplinary change of placement (more than 10 consecutive days, or a pattern totaling more than 10), the IEP team — including you — must meet immediately if possible, and within 10 days of the decision, to answer one question: was the behavior caused by, or directly and substantially related to, the disability — or caused by the district's failure to implement the IEP? That meeting is called a manifestation determination review.
  • If yes: the behavior is a manifestation. The team must conduct a functional behavioral assessment (unless one was already done) and implement a behavior intervention plan (or review and modify the existing one), and your child returns to the prior placement unless you and the district agree otherwise or the narrow exceptions below apply.
  • If no: the district may apply the same discipline as for nondisabled students — but education does not stop. FAPE continues during the removal: services must allow your child to keep participating in the general curriculum and progressing on IEP goals (EC §48915.5(b)).
  • The narrow federal exception. Under federal law (34 CFR 300.530(g)), for weapons, drugs, or infliction of serious bodily injury, the district may place a child in an interim alternative educational setting for up to 45 school days regardless of the manifestation answer. Services continue there too. (Federal rule — California's safeguards notice summarizes discipline more briefly; the 45-day figure is from the federal regulation.)
  • Not yet eligible? A child who does not yet have an IEP can still claim these protections if the district had knowledge the child may have a disability — for example, because you had expressed concern in writing, or requested an evaluation, before the incident. Another reason Step 1 is in writing.

Watch out for: informal removals — "come pick your child up early," repeated shortened days, "home instruction" without an IEP meeting. Days your child is sent home are removal days whether or not the district calls them suspension. Count them, in writing, and use Step 12 when they approach 10.

Step 17: If you are considering a private placement

What this means. Parents who believe the district cannot provide FAPE sometimes enroll a child in a private school and seek reimbursement. The law allows a hearing officer or court to order reimbursement — but notice rules decide many of these cases before the merits are reached.

California specifics. To protect a reimbursement claim, tell the IEP team at the most recent IEP meeting you attended that you reject the proposed placement and intend to enroll privately at public expense — or give the district written notice at least 10 business days (including holidays that fall on business days) before removing your child (EC §§56175–56177). Miss the notice and reimbursement can be reduced or denied even if you win on FAPE. This is a step to take with a special education attorney involved from the start.

Step 18: Age 18 — rights transfer

When your child turns 18 — the age of majority in California — educational rights transfer from you to your child, unless a court has appointed a conservator or your child has chosen to share or delegate decision-making. The district must notify both you and your child of the transfer (the safeguards notice you receive covers this). Plan for it at the transition IEPs in Step 8: some families use supported decision-making agreements or powers of attorney so a parent can keep helping without court involvement. After transfer, you still receive the notices the law sends parents — but the decisions become your adult child's to make.
