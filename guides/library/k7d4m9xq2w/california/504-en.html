The California 504 Guide for Parents

Section 504 plans in California, step by step — including the part nobody tells you: what California adds, and what it doesn't

Education Across the States · Charles Mayfield, Producer Current through November 5, 2026 — next scheduled review July 2027

Read this first: the honest truth about 504 in California

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 is a federal civil rights law. Its education regulations are at 34 CFR Part 104, and they are what runs a 504 plan in California — because California has no separate state 504 system. The California Department of Education (CDE) publishes no state 504 plan process, no state 504 timelines, and no state 504 regulations for K-12 schools. Anyone who tells you a 504 deadline comes from "California law" is mistaken; anyone selling you a "California 504 process" is selling you something that does not exist.

What California does add is real, and this guide covers it: a state discrimination-complaint route (the Uniform Complaint Procedures) that works alongside the federal complaint systems, and district-level structures — a designated 504 coordinator and local board policy — that California districts operate under state governance law. The plan itself, though, is federal from top to bottom. This guide labels every rule with its actual source.

Part 1 — The basics

What a 504 plan is

Section 504 prohibits discrimination based on disability by any program receiving federal funds — which includes every public school district. Under 34 CFR 104.33, a district must provide a free appropriate public education to each qualified student with a disability: regular or special education and related aids and services designed to meet the student's individual educational needs as adequately as the needs of nondisabled students are met.

A 504 plan is the written document recording the accommodations, aids, and services the district will provide. Federal law does not prescribe the plan's format — districts use their own forms — which is why 504 plans look different from district to district, and even from school to school.

Who qualifies — a wider door than the IEP

A student qualifies under Section 504 if they have a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities. Major life activities include learning, reading, concentrating, thinking, communicating, and also major bodily functions (immune, neurological, respiratory, and others). Three things about this door matter:

  • It is broader than IDEA eligibility. There are no categories to fit into. ADHD, anxiety, diabetes, asthma, severe allergies, depression, dyslexia — and many temporary or episodic conditions — can qualify if the substantial-limitation test is met.
  • Episodic conditions count at their worst. A condition that comes and goes (migraine, asthma, many mental health conditions) is judged when it is active, not when it happens to be quiet on evaluation day.
  • Mitigating measures don't count against the student. Except for ordinary eyeglasses or contacts, the district may not consider the helpful effects of medication, devices, or strategies the student uses to cope when deciding whether the impairment substantially limits a major life activity.

Section 504 has no age-3-to-21 band like IDEA; it covers qualified students in the programs of the district. A student can have a 504 plan in preschool programs, elementary, secondary — and 504 protections also reach into postsecondary education under a different subpart.

If your child needs specially designed instruction — not just access supports — the IEP is the right instrument, and the district should be evaluating under IDEA. Schools sometimes steer families toward 504 because it is lighter-weight. Ask the question out loud: "Does my child need specialized instruction, or accommodations?" Then read both guides.

Who runs 504 in a district

Every district with 15 or more employees must designate a Section 504 coordinator responsible for compliance (34 CFR 104.7) and adopt grievance procedures. Most California districts also have a board policy and administrative regulation on 504 (many follow the California School Boards Association's model BP/AR 6164.6). Ask your district office, in writing, two questions: who is our 504 coordinator, and may I have the district's 504 policy and procedural safeguards notice? The answers tell you how seriously the district takes the system.

Part 2 — Getting evaluated

Step 1: Ask for a 504 evaluation — in writing

What this means. Districts have a Child Find duty under Section 504 too: they must annually identify and locate qualified students with disabilities in their jurisdiction who are not receiving a public education (34 CFR 104.32). But as with IDEA, your written request is the surest trigger.

What to do.

  1. Write to the principal or the district's 504 coordinator: "I request that my child be evaluated for eligibility under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act." Describe the impairment and how it affects your child at school — concentration, attendance, stamina, behavior, health needs, output.
  2. Attach what you have: diagnoses, doctor's letters, hospital or therapist notes, teacher comments. A diagnosis alone does not compel eligibility — but the team must consider it.
  3. Date it, keep a copy. If the school suggests an IEP evaluation instead or as well, that is fine — the processes can run together, and the IDEA evaluation carries California's strict timelines.

Step 2: The evaluation — what the law requires (and the deadline it doesn't)

What this means. Under 34 CFR 104.35, before a district takes any action on initial placement, it must conduct an evaluation. The evaluation must:

  • use tests and materials that are validated for the specific purpose used, administered by trained personnel, and tailored to assess specific areas of educational need — not just produce a single IQ-type score;
  • draw information from a variety of sources — aptitude and achievement tests, teacher recommendations, physical condition, social and cultural background, adaptive behavior — and document and carefully consider all of it;
  • be interpreted by a group of persons knowledgeable about the child, the evaluation data, and placement options — the "504 team," which should include you.

The deadline question, answered honestly. Neither federal law nor California law sets a number of days for completing a 504 evaluation or holding the eligibility meeting. There is no 504 equivalent of California's 60-day IEP clock. What exists instead: your district's own policy may set internal timelines, and unexplained drift is itself worth objecting to — in writing, with dates. If the delay is long, ask the district to run an IDEA evaluation in parallel: IDEA's clock is strict, and a child being evaluated under IDEA cannot be left in limbo while a 504 conversation wanders. (If a district tells you a specific number of days "the law" gives it for a 504 evaluation, ask for the citation. There isn't one.)

Consent. Federal 504 regulations do not contain IDEA's written-consent machinery, but districts must give parents notice and an opportunity to participate, and California districts generally obtain written consent for evaluation as standard practice and under their local policies. If a district proposes to evaluate without even notifying you, that is a red flag to raise with the 504 coordinator.

Watch out for: "We don't do 504 evaluations — we do SST meetings" (student study/success teams). An SST can be a useful step, but it is not an evaluation, and it cannot be used as a revolving door that keeps a referred child from ever reaching the 504 team or an IDEA assessment plan.

Step 3: The eligibility decision

What to do.

  1. Attend the team meeting. Ask the team to walk through the data source by source — including yours.
  2. Ask the two legal questions explicitly: (a) Does my child have a physical or mental impairment? (b) Does it substantially limit a major life activity? If the team says the limitation isn't "substantial," ask: compared to most students, and judged without mitigating measures and at the condition's active worst? Those qualifiers decide real cases.
  3. Get the decision and its basis in writing. If your child is found ineligible, you are entitled to notice of the decision and of your procedural safeguards — including your right to challenge it (Part 5).

Watch out for: "Their grades are good, so no substantial limitation." The comparison is to most students in how the major life activity is affected — effort, time, pain, episodes missed, and output all count. A student can be passing and still be substantially limited in concentrating, learning, or a bodily function.

Part 3 — Developing the plan

Step 4: What goes in a 504 plan

Because federal law sets no format, the quality of the writing is the protection. A strong 504 plan names:

  • the impairment and the major life activity limited;
  • each accommodation, aid, or service, specifically: what it is, who provides it, where, how often, and any trigger ("during tests over 30 minutes," "when symptoms flare") — "as needed" without a decider named is a dispute waiting to happen;
  • who is responsible for telling every teacher, including substitutes;
  • for health-related plans, the health procedures (medication, glucose monitoring, seizure or allergy response) and who is trained to perform them;
  • the review date — at least annual review is the standard district practice, and reevaluation must occur periodically and before any significant change in placement (Step 6);
  • how progress or continued need will be judged.

Placement rule (34 CFR 104.34): your child must be educated with nondisabled students to the maximum extent appropriate, including nonacademic and extracurricular activities, unless education in the regular environment with supplementary aids and services cannot be achieved satisfactorily. A 504 plan is almost never a reason to move a child out of general education.

What to do. Read the signed plan against what was said in the meeting, the same day. Anything promised aloud but missing on paper: write to the coordinator the next day, "Per yesterday's meeting, the plan will also include X — please confirm and amend the copy." Keep the reply.

Step 5: Making sure it happens in the classroom

What this means. The district's duty is to implement the plan, not merely to write it. Every teacher who works with your child needs to know the accommodations that apply in their setting — federal law frames implementation as part of providing FAPE under 504, and a teacher who never saw the plan is the district's failure, not the teacher's and not yours.

What to do.

  1. At the start of each term, email each teacher a short introduction and confirm they have the current plan (our downloads include an introduction-letter template pattern in the spec). Keep the replies.
  2. Keep a simple log: date, accommodation, provided or not, impact. Two lines per entry. If the plan works, the log is boring — that is fine. If it doesn't, the log is your evidence.
  3. Raise failures with the 504 coordinator promptly, in writing, while they are fixable. Part 5 is for when prompt and written doesn't work.

Part 4 — Staying involved

Step 6: Reviews and reevaluation

The federal rules (34 CFR 104.35):

  • Periodic reevaluation. The district must reevaluate students with 504 plans periodically. The regulation sets no fixed interval; districts commonly align it to a regular review cycle. If your district's plan form has no review date, ask for one in writing.
  • Reevaluation before a significant change in placement. This is mandatory, and it is the rule that does the work in 504 discipline (Step 8): before the district significantly changes a student's placement — which includes exclusionary discipline of the length described in Step 8 — it must reevaluate.
  • You can trigger review. New diagnosis, medication change, hospitalization, a bad term, a transition between schools: write to the coordinator and ask for a review meeting with the new data.

Watch out for: the plan that follows a child for four years untouched while the child changes around it. A stale plan is a common and quiet failure mode — the annual review ask is yours to make.

Step 7: Records and notices

You have the right to examine relevant records concerning identification, evaluation, and placement (part of the procedural safeguards at 34 CFR 104.36). California's famous five-business-day records rule (Education Code §56504) is written around special education records of students with IEPs; for a 504-only student's records, federal FERPA gives the district up to 45 days to provide access — but ask anyway, in writing, with a date: most districts produce records far faster than the outer federal limit, and your dated request is what makes delay visible.

Part 5 — Rights and disputes

Section 504 gives you four distinct routes. They are not the IDEA ladder: there is no 504 state complaint to CDE's Special Education Division and no 504 due process at OAH — those are IDEA systems. The 504 routes are these:

Step 8 (first, because it surprises people): Discipline under 504 — the rule is reevaluation, not an MDR

National guides get this wrong, so be precise: Section 504's regulations contain no manifestation determination review. The IDEA-style MDR — the meeting asking whether behavior was caused by the disability — is an IDEA procedure. Do not let a district tell you a 504 student's MDR "came out fine," and do not demand an MDR as if Part 104 required one. What Part 104 requires is different and, used correctly, strong:

  • Before a district makes a significant change in placement, it must reevaluate the student (34 CFR 104.35(a)).
  • The U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights (OCR) treats a disciplinary removal as a significant change in placement when it exceeds 10 consecutive school days, or when shorter removals form a pattern totaling more than 10 school days in a year (considering length, total time, and proximity). Before such an exclusion takes effect, the reevaluation must occur — conducted by a knowledgeable group, considering current data.
  • In that reevaluation, the team may consider whether the behavior is connected to the disability and whether the current plan and placement remain appropriate — the substance parents want from an MDR arrives through this door, under its correct legal name.
  • Separately, OCR has long held that disciplining a student for behavior caused by the disability, without reevaluation and reconsideration of placement, can constitute disability discrimination under Section 504.
  • Drugs and alcohol carve-out (federal): Section 504's protections do not cover a student currently engaging in the illegal use of drugs; districts may discipline such conduct as they would for any student (34 CFR 104.3 and the statute). Alcohol and drug addiction as an impairment is treated differently from current illegal use — a nuance worth an attorney's eye if it is your child's situation.

What to do in a discipline crisis: write to the 504 coordinator immediately: "The proposed removal will exceed 10 school days [or completes a pattern exceeding 10]. Under 34 CFR 104.35, the district must reevaluate before this significant change in placement. I request that reevaluation and a meeting before the removal takes effect." Keep the dates of every prior removal — the pattern is arithmetic, and your log from Step 5 is the count.

Step 9: The district's own hearing and grievance process

Federal regulations (34 CFR 104.36) guarantee you, for identification, evaluation, and placement disputes: notice, an opportunity to examine relevant records, an impartial hearing with an opportunity for you to participate and be represented by counsel, and a review procedure. Every district must have these safeguards and a grievance procedure (34 CFR 104.7). Ask the 504 coordinator, in writing, for the district's 504 grievance procedure and hearing request form. Deadlines inside local procedures vary by district — read the district's own document, and treat its stated timelines as the operative ones.

Step 10: The California route — a UCP discrimination complaint

What California adds. Disability discrimination — including denial of 504 rights, harassment, or unequal treatment — can be filed as a Uniform Complaint Procedures (UCP) complaint with your district (5 CCR §4600 et seq.):

  1. File with the district (LEA) within 6 months of the alleged discrimination (or of when you first obtained knowledge of it).
  2. The district investigates and issues a written investigation report within 60 days.
  3. Appeal to CDE within 30 calendar days of the district's report if you disagree (5 CCR §4632). The appeal must include a copy of the original complaint and the district's report. CDE's Equal Opportunity pages publish the process and forms.

Know the boundary: UCP covers discrimination and specified program violations. A pure dispute about the content of a 504 plan (which accommodations, how many minutes) is usually better fought in Steps 9 and 11; a dispute about the district refusing to follow its own nondiscrimination duties is UCP-shaped. And remember the mirror-image rule from our IEP guide: IDEA compliance complaints are no longer UCP complaints — since 2020 they go to CDE's Special Education Division under 5 CCR §§3200–3205.

Step 11: The federal route — a complaint to the Office for Civil Rights (OCR)

What this means. OCR, in the U.S. Department of Education, enforces Section 504 in schools. Anyone may file.

  • Deadline: 180 days from the last act of discrimination (OCR may waive the deadline for good cause in some circumstances). If you first use the district's grievance process, the clock rules change: file with OCR within 60 days after the district process concludes.
  • How: file online through OCR's complaint portal (linked from ed.gov's OCR pages), or by email (OCR@ed.gov) or the OCR hotline, 1-800-421-3481. You do not have to finish the district's process before filing with OCR.
  • What OCR does: OCR reviews the complaint, may investigate, and can require the district to enter a resolution agreement with corrective steps and monitoring. OCR does not award damages, and it is not fast — but districts take OCR findings seriously.
  • A 2025 reality note: OCR reorganized its regional offices in 2025, and the San Francisco office that historically served California was among those closed. Do not rely on old guides (or old bookmarks) for a California OCR mailing address — file online through the portal, which routes the complaint correctly regardless of office assignments.

Step 12: Court — and retaliation protection

Section 504 claims may also be brought in court; families usually do this with an attorney, sometimes alongside ADA claims. Two closing protections matter to every parent in this guide:

  • Retaliation is illegal. Section 504 and the ADA prohibit retaliation against anyone for asserting disability rights — yours or your child's. If treatment changes after you advocate, document it and treat it as its own complaint (Steps 10–11).
  • Your child with an IEP is also covered by 504. The laws overlap; the IEP guide and this guide are companions, not alternatives.

Where to get help tonight

  • Your district's Section 504 coordinator — name and contact from the district office; ask for the district's 504 policy and safeguards notice in the same call.
  • OCR: online complaint portal via ed.gov · OCR@ed.gov · 1-800-421-3481
