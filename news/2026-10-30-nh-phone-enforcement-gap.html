In New Hampshire, a 30-Point Gap on Whether Phone Rules Are Enforced Consistently

October 30, 2026 · By Charles Mayfield, Executive Producer

HomeShowsNew Hampshire

New Hampshire has its first real evidence on its bell-to-bell cell phone law, ahead of the annual review each district policy requires. Under RSA 189:1-a, V, school boards and chartered public school boards must adopt a policy prohibiting students from using personal communication devices from the first bell that starts instructional time until the dismissal bell that ends the school day. The law took effect July 1, 2025, enacted as part of the state budget bill signed June 27, 2025.

Earlier this year, the RAND Corporation and the National Education Association surveyed public school teachers and principals about personal device policies; the NEA published its account of the results on October 29, 2026. More than half of both groups said their school used clear and consistent rules and clearly defined exceptions, but on consistent enforcement they split 30 points apart: 87% of principals said their school used it, while only 57% of teachers said the same. Fewer than half of teachers said their school used regular feedback and evaluation mechanisms.

Principals reported positive impact on nine of ten measures, with 85% reporting improvement in student distractibility. Teachers reported less distractibility (63%) and more time for teaching (49%), but only 46% reported improved academic performance and 37% less cyberbullying — in teachers’ judgment, attention improved first, and deeper climate and learning effects are still unproven. A Concord high school social studies teacher, also president of the Concord Education Association, told the association that hallways once silent with students staring at phones are now filled with conversation and students are doing better work.

The exceptions are narrow, not optional. A policy cannot prohibit device use to support learning when it is identified in a student’s IEP or Section 504 plan; the same protection covers medical needs — insulin pumps, glucose sensors — and language access for emergent multilingual learners, with exceptions approved through the superintendent or a designee. If a device is written into an IEP or Section 504 plan, taking the device away is not enforcement: it is a failure to implement the plan.

Takeaway: The phones are away, and attention is better for it. At the annual review, ask teachers directly whether enforcement looks the same from classroom to classroom, audit the exceptions, and close the feedback loop fewer than half of teachers reported having — the show’s recommended test for the work now: consistency, and exceptions handled exactly as written.
