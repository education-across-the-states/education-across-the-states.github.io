In New Hampshire, the Total Barely Moved — the Money Did

November 2, 2026 · By Charles Mayfield, Executive Producer

HomeShowsNew HampshireSubscribe

New Hampshire’s adequate education aid total barely moved this year — and underneath that flat total, the money moved a lot. As today’s New Hampshire Education Daily reports, the New Hampshire Fiscal Policy Institute, analyzing New Hampshire Department of Education municipal aid data, puts total adequate education aid at about $1.079 billion in state fiscal year 2026, close to the prior year. Inside that nearly flat total, 145 municipalities received less state adequacy aid than in 2025, and 99 received more.

One mechanism drove most of the losses: the phase-out of Hold Harmless Grants, which protected towns from losing aid when the formula changed. Under current law those grants step down by 20 percent every two years — from $59.4 million statewide in the 2024–25 biennium, to $47.6 million in 2026–27, to $35.7 million in 2028–29 — with the phase-out complete by fiscal year 2034–35. Of the 134 municipalities that receive Hold Harmless Grants, 109 did not receive enough Extraordinary Needs Grant funding to offset the 20 percent reduction, and 83 of those received less total aid overall. The largest dollar decreases were in Derry, Laconia, Concord, and Weare; in Derry, the Hold Harmless Grant fell by $903,727 and the Extraordinary Needs Grant fell by $627,615, a combined loss of about $1.53 million.

The same episode opens with a deadline that has now passed. The annual restraint and seclusion report under New Hampshire law, RSA 126-U:8, II, was due on November 1. It must go to the chairs of the House and Senate education committees, with the number and location of reported incidents and the status of any outstanding investigations. Checked again on the morning of Monday, November 2, the new report had still not appeared in the published record we can verify — one day past the statutory date. We will keep checking, and we will report what the report shows when it is out.

Tomorrow, voters take up Question 2, which asks each district whether to cap the local response for two years. If adopted by a three-fifths majority, counted across every town in the district, the school property tax levy could grow only with inflation plus new construction, and SAU central office spending could not exceed six percent of total appropriations, for fiscal years 2028 and 2029; spending above the cap would need a separate override vote, also at three-fifths. On November 15, the Department of Education posts the first adequacy aid estimates for fiscal year 2028 — the first numbers districts will use to budget under the result.

Takeaway: Find your municipality in the split, and identify whether Hold Harmless, Extraordinary Needs, or both moved for you — the reason is in the components. Model the next Hold Harmless step now, not in two years, and do not draft a fiscal year 2028 budget until tomorrow’s Question 2 result is paired with the November 15 state estimate: the vote sets the local rules, the estimate sets the state number, and you need both on the same page.
