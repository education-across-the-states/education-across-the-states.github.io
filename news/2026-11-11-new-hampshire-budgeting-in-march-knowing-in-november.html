Budgeting in March, Knowing in November: New Hampshire’s Tax Cap Calendar Problem

November 11, 2026 · By Charles Mayfield, Executive Producer

HomeShowsNew HampshireSubscribe

The hardest part of New Hampshire's new school tax cap may not be the limit itself. It may be the calendar. As today's NH Education Daily reports, the New Hampshire Bulletin reported November 10 that, under Department of Revenue Administration guidance issued Friday, the department will set the final tax rates in November 2027. For a capped district, that rate is built from the prior year's tax levy, plus inflation, plus growth from new taxable property — months after school budgets are voted in March.

In Pelham, the squeeze is already concrete. Its board chair said health care costs through HealthTrust, the district's insurer, climbed 14 percent this year, and transportation costs are expected to rise 20 percent next year, against a cap inflation allowance he put at about 3.7 percent. Salaries and benefits make up 70 to 80 percent of a typical Pelham budget and are mostly locked in collective bargaining agreements; special education services are mandated by law, and transportation is required for kindergarten through eighth grade students. In his assessment, a cut therefore becomes a staffing decision, ideally through attrition rather than layoffs.

Districts also do not agree yet on what the new statute requires. The superintendent in Fremont said if you put twenty superintendents in a room, you would probably get five different answers, because five different lawyers are advising those twenty districts — and staffing choices have to be wrapped up by January, when public hearings and deliberative sessions start. Fremont has one cushion: starting next year it will stop tuitioning its high school students to Sanborn Regional High School and offer cheaper choices instead, a change expected to save about $500,000 phased over at least four years, though the superintendent warned that may spare Fremont only for the cap's first year. Middleton is the counterexample: its board chair cited a cost per pupil of $19,699.60 in 2024–25, below the state average of $22,700, and believes it can meet the cap without further staff reductions.

On Thursday, November 12, Winchester holds its tax cap recount and the State Board holds hearings in Concord. On Sunday, November 15, the Department of Education's first adequacy aid estimates for fiscal year 2028 are due — the first state numbers districts will plug into these budgets. The Manchester recount, on a petition filed November 10, must be held between November 15 and November 20.

Takeaway: Build the March budget on a conservative levy estimate and write down every assumption behind it, so a fall correction is a planned adjustment, not a surprise. Make staffing decisions on the January clock, not the November tax-rate clock, and decide now whether an override — which needs a 60 percent vote — belongs in your strategy.
