Education Across the States — Briefing

McKinney-Vento: The Students the Law Already Covers

What the law requires, where districts get it wrong, and what full compliance looks like in policy and practice

Home States Shows Resources Law Library Press Kit

The McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act's education subtitle — the Education for Homeless Children and Youths program, 42 U.S.C. §§ 11431–11435, as amended by the Every Student Succeeds Act — is a barrier-removal and school-stability law. It is not a housing program, and it is not a program only for districts that win a federal subgrant. Its core duties — identify, enroll immediately, keep the student in the school of origin when that is in the student's best interest, transport on request, provide comparable services, and resolve disputes enroll-first — bind every local education agency in a state receiving program funds, whether or not the agency receives a subgrant.

It is also one of the most widely misunderstood laws in American education. Most students it covers are not in shelters. Most compliance failures are not exotic: they are a front desk asking for a lease, a transportation office waiting on another district, a policy manual that has not been opened since before the 2016 amendments. This briefing states what the law says, documents the failures with the audit and court record, and then sets out the compliance build — the board policy, identification system, and daily procedures that keep a district on the right side of all three.

Who qualifies

The statute defines "homeless children and youths" as individuals who lack a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence (42 U.S.C. § 11434a(2)). Those are three separate tests, and a living situation can fail any one of them: not fixed (not stationary or permanent, or the family has no legal right to stay), not regular (not used on a predictable, routine basis — couch-surfing among several homes), or not adequate (no water, electricity, or heat; infested; no working kitchen or toilet; unreasonably dangerous). The definition expressly includes children and youth who are:

  • Sharing the housing of other persons due to loss of housing, economic hardship, or a similar reason — "doubled-up," the largest category by far. The reason for sharing is the test, not the host's generosity, a lease, or how long the arrangement has lasted.
  • Living in motels, hotels, trailer parks, or camping grounds due to the lack of alternative adequate accommodations — including motels the family pays for itself. Who pays is not the test; the lack of an alternative is.
  • Living in emergency or transitional shelters, or abandoned in hospitals.
  • Living in a place not designed for or ordinarily used as regular sleeping accommodation — and, expressly, in cars, parks, public spaces, abandoned buildings, substandard housing, and bus or train stations.
  • Migratory children who qualify because they are living in one of the circumstances above. Migrant status alone is not enough.

An unaccompanied youth — a homeless child or youth not in the physical custody of a parent or guardian (§ 11434a(6)) — may be doubled-up, in a shelter, or unsheltered; the categories overlap. And "enroll" and "enrollment" (§ 11434a(1)) mean attending classes and participating fully in school activities. A student kept out of athletics, activities, or class itself while paperwork is sorted out is not enrolled in the eyes of this law.

Do not substitute the housing definition. The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development's definition for its housing programs generally reaches shelter, street, and place-not-meant-for-habitation situations and does not include doubled-up families or most self-paid motel stays. In 2023–24, only 12.71% of students identified by schools were in shelters or transitional housing when first identified — most McKinney-Vento-eligible families are not eligible for HUD homeless assistance. Schools must apply the education definition to education rights, regardless of HUD status. Applying the HUD definition in a school is the single most consequential eligibility error a district can make.

The core rights

School of origin, and the best-interest determination

The school of origin (§ 11432(g)(3)(I)) is the school the student attended when permanently housed or was last enrolled in — including a preschool — and, when the student completes that school's final grade, the designated receiving school at the next grade level. The district must, according to the student's best interest, either keep the student in the school of origin for the duration of homelessness and for the remainder of the academic year if the student becomes permanently housed during that year, or enroll the student in the local school for the attendance area where the student is actually living (§ 11432(g)(3)(A)).

The best-interest determination (§ 11432(g)(3)(B)) has a structure, and each part is mandatory: the district must presume that keeping the student in the school of origin is in the student's best interest, except when contrary to the request of the parent, guardian, or unaccompanied youth; it must consider student-centered factors, including the impact of mobility on achievement, education, health, and safety, giving priority to that request; and if it decides against the school of origin or the requested school, it must provide a written explanation, in a form the family or youth can understand, including the right to appeal. For an unaccompanied youth, the liaison must assist in the decision, give priority to the youth's views, and give notice of the right to appeal. Cost, distance, and administrative convenience are not student-centered factors, and the statute contains no mileage or time limit on school-of-origin rights.

Immediate enrollment — no documents required first

The school selected shall immediately enroll the student even if the student cannot produce records normally required for enrollment — prior academic records, immunization and other health records, proof of residency, or other documentation — and even if the student missed application or enrollment deadlines during a period of homelessness (§ 11432(g)(3)(C)). U.S. Department of Education guidance and SchoolHouse Connection state the same rule for birth certificates and proof of guardianship. The enrolling school must immediately contact the school last attended to obtain records; missing immunizations are referred to the liaison, who assists in obtaining them — enrollment is not held while either happens. Holding a student in a shelter "transitional classroom," or sending a family away to return with documents, is prohibited. Removing barriers is a freestanding, continuing duty: states and districts must review and revise any policy that operates as a barrier, including barriers from outstanding fees or fines and from absences (§§ 11432(g)(1)(I), (g)(7)).

Transportation — including the 50/50 default

Transportation to and from the school of origin must be provided at the request of the parent or guardian — or, for an unaccompanied youth, the liaison (§ 11432(g)(1)(J)(iii)). If the student keeps living in the area served by the district where the school of origin sits, that district provides or arranges it. If the student has moved into another district's area while continuing in the school of origin, the two districts must agree on a method to apportion responsibility and costs — and if they cannot agree, responsibility and costs are shared equally. Department of Education guidance confirms the equal-split rule holds even across state lines. The duty exists even if the district provides no transportation to housed students; it continues during enrollment disputes; it continues through the end of the school year in which the student becomes permanently housed; it must be arranged promptly; and where lack of transportation to extracurricular activities is a barrier, that must be cured too, because enrollment includes full participation. The excess cost of school-of-origin transportation may be defrayed from program subgrant funds and from the Title I reservation described below.

The liaison — every district, real capacity

Every local education agency must designate an appropriate staff person, able to carry out the statutory duties, as its liaison for homeless children and youths (§ 11432(g)(1)(J)(ii)) — whether or not the agency receives a subgrant. "Able to carry out the duties" is a capacity standard, not a title. The liaison's duties (§ 11432(g)(6)(A)) are to ensure that homeless children and youths are identified through outreach and coordination; are enrolled and have a full and equal opportunity to succeed; have access to Head Start, IDEA Part C early intervention, and other preschool programs; receive referrals to health care, dental, mental health, housing, and other services; that parents and guardians are informed of educational opportunities and given meaningful ways to participate; that public notice of educational rights is disseminated where families and youth actually are — schools, shelters, public libraries, and soup kitchens — in understandable form; that enrollment disputes are mediated; that families and youth are fully informed of, and assisted in accessing, transportation; that school personnel receive professional development and support; and that unaccompanied youth are enrolled, can meet the same academic standards including through credit-accrual procedures, and are informed of their status as independent students for federal student aid, with the liaison's verification for the FAFSA. Liaisons must also take part in the professional development the State Coordinator provides, and the state must publish an annually updated list of liaisons.

Disputes: enroll first

If a dispute arises over eligibility, school selection, or enrollment, the student shall be immediately enrolled in the school in which enrollment is sought, pending final resolution of the dispute, including all available appeals (§ 11432(g)(3)(E)). The family or youth must receive a written explanation of the decision, including the rights to appeal, and must be referred to the liaison, who carries out the dispute process as expeditiously as possible. All services the student is eligible for — including school-of-origin transportation on request — continue while the dispute runs. Disputes between districts are resolved at the state level. The student never waits for the adults to finish.

Comparable services, free meals, and the Title I set-aside

Each homeless student must receive services comparable to those offered to other students in the school selected (§ 11432(g)(4)): transportation; educational services the student is eligible for, including Title I, programs for children with disabilities, and programs for English learners; career and technical education; gifted and talented programs; and school nutrition programs. Homeless students are categorically eligible for free school meals with no application — a list or statement from the liaison or a shelter director is sufficient documentation, certification must happen as soon as possible and no later than three operating days after the district receives documentation (7 C.F.R. § 245.6), and eligibility lasts the rest of the school year plus up to 30 operating days into the next. Separately, every district receiving Title I, Part A funds must reserve funds as necessary to serve homeless students (20 U.S.C. § 6313(c)(3)) — sized by a needs assessment done in consultation with the liaison, not by formula, and usable for purposes including part or all of the liaison's salary and the excess cost of school-of-origin transportation. Districts must also have procedures to award full and partial credit for coursework satisfactorily completed at a prior school (§ 11432(g)(1)(F)(ii)), and information about a student's living situation is a student education record under FERPA — never directory information (§ 11432(g)(3)(G)).

The overlap with special education

IDEA incorporates the McKinney-Vento definition (20 U.S.C. § 1401(11); 34 C.F.R. § 300.19), and IDEA child find expressly includes homeless children, infants, toddlers, and preschoolers. Expedited evaluations of a homeless student's strengths and needs are an expressly fundable activity (§ 11433(d)(2)); mobility is not a lawful reason to restart or stall an evaluation. For an unaccompanied homeless youth, the agency must protect the child's IDEA rights, including assignment of a surrogate parent where required (34 C.F.R. § 300.519(a)(4)). One caution the sources are explicit about: IDEA's federal initial-evaluation timeline is 60 days from parental consent or the state-established timeframe (34 C.F.R. § 300.301). There is no freestanding federal 30-day homeless evaluation rule — 30-day figures in some state materials are state rules, not federal ones.

Foster care is not McKinney-Vento

One sentence in older district documents causes outsized damage: "awaiting foster care placement." The Every Student Succeeds Act removed that phrase from the McKinney-Vento definition, effective December 10, 2016. Any policy, poster, or enrollment form that still lists it as McKinney-Vento eligibility is out of date. Since that date, educational stability for children in foster care has been governed by a different law — Title I, Part A of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act as amended by ESSA (20 U.S.C. §§ 6311(g)(1)(E), 6312(c)(5)) — with a different local contact and a different transportation cost rule.

The practical rule cuts both ways: do not apply the foster-care transportation cost rule to a homeless student, and do not deny a child in foster care immediate enrollment because the child is "not McKinney-Vento." A student can move between the two systems within a single school year, and the handoff has to be planned so there is no gap in stability, enrollment, or transportation.

Where districts get it wrong

The failures below recur across federal and state monitoring, state audits, a federal inspector general review, national surveys, and litigation. Each one maps to a specific duty in the statute.

"Doubled-up families aren't really homeless"

The most common and most damaging error: applying the HUD definition, or inventing local tests the statute does not contain — requiring a landlord's letter, contacting the host or the landlord to verify, treating an arrangement as ineligible because it has "lasted too long," or telling a family paying for a motel room that it is housed. Doubled-up students are 74.3% of everyone identified. The National Center for Homeless Education's eligibility guidance states that the liaison holds final determination authority, that facts are gathered privately, and that invasive verification — contacting landlords, surveillance, interviewing neighbors — is prohibited; contacting a landlord about a family's living situation also violates FERPA.

"We have no shelter, so we have no homeless students"

Most homeless students are not in shelters, and identification duties bind every district. In a national post-ESSA survey, only 17% of surveyed states reported that all of their districts identified homeless students; 83% reported districts identifying zero — anywhere from 1 to 418 such districts per state. A data investigation by the Center for Public Integrity found roughly 8,000 districts failing a one-in-twenty benchmark — the share of students in poverty a district would ordinarily be expected to identify — in both of two school years analyzed. Federal guidance treats the number of districts identifying zero as a capacity warning, and state monitors are instructed to cross-check identification against poverty data to flag districts that under-identify. A count of zero is a red flag for non-identification, not evidence of absence.

The California audit: what under-identification looks like up close

The California State Auditor (Report 2019-104, November 2019) found the state's schools undercounted homeless youth by at least 37% in 2017–18 — about 270,000 reported against at least 370,000 likely. Of the six districts reviewed in depth: two did not administer an annual housing questionnaire at all; none had trained all the staff who are in a position to identify homeless youth; only one disseminated rights information in the public places the law requires; and board policies were years out of date — two had not been updated since 2003 and most did not reflect the 2016 ESSA changes. State monitoring, the audit found, verified only that a policy existed, without checking whether it was current.

Enrollment delayed for documents — now with damages

Requiring proof of residency, records, immunization histories, birth certificates, or guardianship papers before enrollment violates the immediate-enrollment duty, and "enrollment" delayed is enrollment denied: the word means attending classes and participating fully. In Martin v. Piserchia (U.S. District Court for the District of New Jersey, 2024), a court found a district violated McKinney-Vento by refusing to enroll during a residency dispute based on its own unilateral fact-finding, failing to provide a written explanation and appeal rights, and failing to follow the dispute process — exposing the district to monetary damages. In Core v. Chicago Board of Education (N.D. Ill., August 30, 2024), a McKinney-Vento claim by a homeless student with a disability, alleging enrollment denial for lack of records and delays to an IEP, survived in part past a motion to dismiss. And in September 2026, Alabama education officials sued the state athletic association over an unaccompanied homeless youth barred from athletics — litigating the principle that exclusion from activities is non-enrollment.

Transportation standoffs and invented limits

"The other district won't pay," "it's too far," and "we don't bus anyone" are not lawful answers. The cross-district default is an equal split; there is no mileage or time cap in the statute; the duty applies where housed students get no busing; and the ride continues during disputes and after permanent housing through the end of the school year. The Department of Education's guidance recommends pre-negotiated inter-district agreements precisely because ad hoc cost disputes recur — cost is resolved between agencies, with state help, never by suspending the ride.

The rest of the pattern

  • A liaison in name only — a full-time administrator with no allocated time, no training, no budget, and no presence in the buildings. The statute requires someone able to carry out ten clusters of duties, who takes part in state training and ensures school personnel are trained.
  • A $0 Title I set-aside because "we identified no one" — the reservation is required of every Title I district and sized by a needs assessment with the liaison. A zero produced by a broken identification system does not make a zero reservation defensible.
  • Privacy breaches — treating living-situation information as directory information, or sharing it with landlords, housing authorities, or neighbors without consent. It is a non-directory education record under FERPA.
  • Preschoolers ignored — preschool is inside the Act: it counts in the school of origin, liaison duties include Head Start and IDEA Part C access, and younger siblings of identified students are a primary identification lead. Subgrant-served children ages 0–5 numbered 84,883 in 2023–24, and infants and toddlers remain structurally under-represented.
  • Credits denied at transfer — seat-time-only rules that erase partial coursework are a named barrier; the law requires procedures for full and partial credit.
  • Eligibility treated as ending at the lease signing — school-of-origin attendance and transportation run through the end of that school year.
  • Conditions that drive families away — conditioning enrollment on guardianship papers, parental presence, or a child-welfare report. Homelessness alone is not a basis for a report, and contact that creates a barrier to enrollment or retention violates the Act. (Mandatory reporting of suspected abuse or neglect under state law is a separate duty — it is not an enrollment precondition.)

The compliance build

None of what follows requires inventing anything. The model sources are the National Center for Homeless Education's liaison toolkit and State Coordinators' Handbook and SchoolHouse Connection's LEA policy checklist and quick reference, layered over the district's own state coordinator guidance. A district that builds these six pieces has, in substance, built the system monitors come looking for.

1. Board policy with actual contents

A one-paragraph "the district will comply with McKinney-Vento" policy is the model the California audit treated as failure. Board policy and administrative procedure together should contain: (1) the statutory definition verbatim, in its current text — no "awaiting foster care placement" — plus definitions of unaccompanied youth, school of origin (including preschool and the feeder school), and enrollment; (2) an equal-access, non-segregation, non-stigmatization commitment; (3) liaison designation by position, with authority, an allocated-time capacity standard, the statutory duty list, and a succession procedure; (4) identification procedures and the liaison's final authority on eligibility; (5) school selection and the best-interest determination, including the presumption, the factor list, and the written-explanation requirement; (6) immediate enrollment with no document bars and full participation from day one; (7) transportation, including the request procedure, the inter-district agreement, and the 50/50 default; (8) dispute resolution — enroll first, written explanation, liaison referral, state appeal; (9) comparable services, categorical Title I eligibility, and direct certification for free meals; (10) a scheduled barrier review — at least annual — of enrollment, fees and fines, attendance, discipline, uniform, and activity rules as they affect homeless students; (11) privacy protections for living-situation information; (12) preschool, credit-accrual, and college-readiness procedures; (13) coordination clauses for Title I, special education, child nutrition, and the foster-care point of contact, kept distinct from the liaison; (14) training and public-notice requirements; and (15) data and reporting procedures, including internal checks on zero counts.

2. A liaison with documented capacity

Designate by position description, with the statutory duties, decision authority on eligibility and best-interest determinations, and direct access to transportation, Title I, special education, child nutrition, and data staff. Set the allocated time from the Department of Education's capacity factors: the identified count and its trend, the homeless count as a share of students in poverty, the number of buildings, dispute and transportation volume, and monitoring findings — and write the determination down. Larger districts fund building-level points of contact trained by the liaison; the Title I reservation may pay part or all of the liaison's salary. Name and train a backup, so turnover does not zero out the function. Publish the liaison's name and contact information where the law expects it found: the state's annually updated list, district and school websites, enrollment packets, rights posters, and handbooks.

3. An identification system, not a hope

  • A housing questionnaire for every student, every year, embedded in enrollment and registration and repeated at any mid-year enrollment — written in plain language mirroring the statute's living situations, and never headlined with the word "homeless," which families fear signing. State that answers are confidential and used to determine eligibility for support; provide the major languages of the district and oral assistance.
  • Same-day routing. Every possible response goes to the liaison the same day. Front-office staff screen and refer; they do not adjudicate.
  • Mid-year triggers: address changes, attendance patterns, transportation requests, counselor, nurse, and social-worker referrals, shelter and motel addresses, and sibling identification — identifying one child triggers a check for siblings, including children under five.
  • A determination protocol that applies the fixed, regular, and adequate tests and the reason for sharing — decided by the liaison, documented by category and date, without landlord contacts, surveillance, or document demands a family cannot safely meet. A denial is a disputable decision and carries written explanation and appeal rights.
  • Data flags with privacy controls: a student-information-system flag carrying the nighttime-residence category, unaccompanied status, school-of-origin outcome, transportation arrangement, and identification date — role-based access only, driving automatic service triggers (meal certification, Title I services, transportation, fee waivers), never a label in classrooms or on public lists.
  • Termly self-audits: zero-identification buildings checked; year-to-year swings reviewed (federal reviewers have flagged changes of 10% or more); the identified rate compared against the district's poverty context — a homeless rate far below it is an under-identification signal, not a success.

4. Front-office practice

Enrollment denials happen at counters, not in board meetings, so the front office gets a one-page script and checklist: if the questionnaire or the conversation suggests eligibility — enroll today, in classes, with full participation; call the liaison the same day; request records from the prior school immediately; refer immunization gaps to the liaison; issue fee waivers, supplies, and meal certification the same day; and arrange interim transportation without waiting for the inter-district cost conversation. Replace the standard document demands for this population with instruments designed not to become new barriers — an affidavit of residence, a caregiver affidavit, an unaccompanied-youth enrollment form — and accept records directly from families. Within the first week, run the post-enrollment sweep: best-interest confirmation, transportation working in both directions, meal certification confirmed within three operating days, Title I services, special-education and Section 504 status including any evaluation in progress, a credit audit for secondary students, activity and athletics eligibility, and a named school contact for the family or youth.

5. Transportation agreements signed before the case arrives

Negotiate standing agreements with every neighboring district — and, where relevant, across state lines — before a student needs one: who arranges and who pays; the statutory 50/50 default where the agreement is silent or a specific case cannot be agreed; modes from existing routes to contracted vehicles and transit passes; an arrangement-time standard measured in school days, with interim transport; invoicing cadence; escalation to the state coordinator; and an express clause that no dispute suspends transportation. Give the liaison and the transportation office a shared request-and-tracking workflow, train drivers and dispatchers on the rights and on confidentiality, and budget the excess cost explicitly across general funds, subgrant funds, and the Title I reservation, tracked per student so the set-aside needs assessment rests on real numbers.

6. Training, notice, and coordination

Train at least annually every staff member positioned to identify or enroll — enrollment staff, attendance officers, principals, counselors, teachers, paraprofessionals, specialized instructional support personnel, bus drivers — on the definition with doubled-up and motel examples, the signs of homelessness, the referral steps, the enrollment rules, and confidentiality; document agendas and attendance, because monitors ask for both. Disseminate notice of rights where the statute names — schools, shelters, public libraries, soup kitchens — and where families and youth actually are: motels, laundromats, food pantries, clinics, public-housing offices, and youth gathering points, in plain language and in translation, with liaison and State Coordinator contacts. Then run the coordination as standing routines with named partners: the liaison co-signs the Title I set-aside needs assessment; special education runs a joint child-find and expedited-evaluation protocol and keeps a surrogate-parent roster ready; child nutrition certifies from the liaison's list the same day; the foster-care point of contact and the liaison operate separate-but-coordinated roles with a written handoff checklist; and housing agencies, shelters, Continuums of Care, Head Start, and runaway and homeless youth programs work under two-way referral protocols, with any data sharing kept inside FERPA.

What monitors will ask a district to prove. State coordinators are required to monitor districts, and the recurring indicator list is consistent across state protocols: a designated liaison with a position description, allotted time, and completed annual training — applied to all districts, grant or no grant; a current board policy and dispute-resolution procedure with evidence of barrier review; the housing questionnaire in the enrollment packet, identification counts read against poverty data, and an explanation for zero counts; best-interest forms and evidence families were told their rights; transportation records, inter-district agreements, and cost-sharing records showing rides provided on request; dispute records showing students enrolled and transported during resolution, plus a log of barriers and complaints; posters, webpages, and distribution locations for public notice; the Title I set-aside amount, how it was determined, and the liaison's involvement; service and referral records, meal-certification timeliness, and special-education coordination records; privacy storage and access controls; timely, accurate federal data submissions; and outcome indicators — chronic absence, attendance, and graduation for homeless students against their peers. A district that assembles that file before it is asked has already built the system.

Funding, and what changed in 2024–2026

Federal funding for the program has been flat at $129 million for four consecutive years — FY2023, FY2024, FY2025, and the enacted FY2026 appropriation (passed February 3, 2026) — while the identified student count rose 28.5% over two school years. At that level, only about 20% of districts receive a program subgrant. The one-time $800 million American Rescue Plan homeless children and youth program reached more than half of districts before its obligation deadline of September 30, 2024; its expiration is the funding cliff behind much of the current liaison-capacity strain.

The FY2026 presidential budget proposed eliminating the program as a standalone line by folding it into a consolidated block grant. Congress rejected the consolidation in both chambers — the Senate Appropriations Committee advanced level funding 26–3 on July 31, 2025 — and the final FY2026 law kept the $129 million, required prompt release of funds and sufficient departmental staffing, continued the bar on transferring programs to other agencies without express congressional authorization, and directed the Department of Education to issue guidance allowing program funds for short-term emergency motel stays, case by case, as a last resort. Whether that guidance has been issued could not be verified from the sources behind this briefing. The administration has separately announced a transfer of the office that houses the program to the Department of Labor, and litigation by 20 state attorneys general and the District of Columbia over department firings and transfers was ongoing in the latest sources reviewed; its status after February 2026 is not verified here. The Homeless Children and Youth Act — which would let families verified under the education definition be assessed for housing assistance — was re-introduced in 2025 (S. 1667; H.R. 6403) and has not been enacted; it would not change education rights in any event. The bottom line for practice: no enacted change to students' McKinney-Vento rights occurred in 2024–2026. The duties on this page are the duties in force.
